It's Sunday night, and your husband is shaking so badly he can't hold his coffee cup. He swears he's done drinking, starting right now. Your mother-in-law says, "Good, let him tough it out." Your sister texts a link about medical detox. Your teenager asks quietly, "Is Dad going to be okay?" And you're standing in the kitchen with no idea who's right.

Medical detox is one of the most misunderstood steps in getting help for addiction. Some families think it's optional, something only for "really bad" cases. Others think detox *is* the treatment, and that once the shaking stops the problem is solved. Both ideas can put a loved one at risk, and both tend to split families into camps right when they need to act together.

This guide is for the whole family: parents, spouses, siblings, adult children and grandparents. We'll cover what medical detox is, when stopping at home can be dangerous, what to ask a detox program, and how your family can share one plan so detox becomes a first step instead of a revolving door.

## What is medical detox?

Medical detox is supervised care that helps a person safely get through withdrawal, the physical and emotional symptoms that happen when someone stops a substance their body has become dependent on. Doctors and nurses monitor vital signs, treat symptoms with medication when needed and step in quickly if something goes wrong.

Think of medical detox as clearing the runway, not flying the plane. On its own, it rarely changes the habits and relationships that keep addiction going.

### Detox and withdrawal: two terms worth knowing

- **Withdrawal** is what the body goes through when a substance is stopped or cut back sharply. It can range from irritability and poor sleep to seizures.

- **Dependence** means the body has adapted to having the substance and reacts when it's gone. Dependence and addiction often travel together, but they aren't the same thing.

## Can someone safely detox at home?

Sometimes, but not always, and families shouldn't make that call on their own. Withdrawal from alcohol and from sedatives such as benzodiazepines (Xanax, Valium, Klonopin and similar drugs) can cause seizures and a dangerous condition called delirium tremens, which can be fatal. Anyone who has been drinking heavily every day, or taking these medications regularly, should talk to a doctor before stopping.

Opioid withdrawal is rarely life-threatening by itself, but it is miserable, and that misery drives many people straight back to using. After even a few days without opioids, a person's tolerance drops. If they use their old amount, the risk of overdose climbs sharply.

### Warning signs that call for medical help

- Shaking, sweating or a racing heart within hours of the last drink

- Confusion, hallucinations or seeing or hearing things that aren't there

- A history of seizures or past withdrawal that went badly

- Heavy daily use of alcohol, benzodiazepines or several substances at once

- Other health problems such as heart disease, diabetes, pregnancy or serious depression

In more than 20 years of working with families, some of the scariest calls I've taken started with "He decided to quit cold turkey this weekend." The willingness was real. The plan just wasn't safe.

## Why does medical detox divide families?

Families split over medical detox because everyone is reacting to a different fear. One person fears losing control, another fears the cost, another fears the person will refuse and never ask again. When those fears go unspoken, they come out as arguments about whether detox is "really necessary."

"He did detox before, and it didn't work." "We can handle this ourselves." Each is understandable. None is a medical opinion. The fastest way through is to agree that a doctor or detox professional decides what level of care is safe, and the family decides how to support that decision together.

## What should we ask a medical detox program?

A good detox program will welcome your questions and answer them plainly. Ask about medical staffing, what happens after detox and whether they help with the next step. If a program can't explain its discharge plan, keep looking.

### Questions for the first phone call

1. Is there a doctor or nurse practitioner overseeing care, and are nurses on site around the clock?

2. Which substances do you manage withdrawal from, and do you offer medications to ease symptoms?

3. For opioids, do you offer **medication for opioid use disorder** (MOUD), such as buprenorphine, methadone or naltrexone, or help connecting to it after discharge?

4. How long is a typical stay, and what insurance do you accept?

5. What happens on the last day? Do you arrange the next level of care before discharge?

6. How will you communicate with family, and what does my loved one need to sign so you can talk with us?

Privacy laws mean staff often can't share anything without written permission, so ask your loved one, early and kindly, to sign a release for one or two family contacts.

## What happens after medical detox?

The days right after medical detox are some of the most vulnerable in early recovery. The body feels better, cravings can still be strong, and the person may feel ready to "take it from here."

Ideally, your loved one moves straight from detox into the next level of care. That might be residential treatment (living at a facility), a partial hospitalization or intensive outpatient program (structured treatment during the day while living at home or in sober housing), or ongoing outpatient counseling and medication. The detox team can recommend which fits.

### The gap is where many people slip

When there's a gap of even a few days between detox and treatment, old routines rush back in. Families can help close that gap by having the [next level of care](/blog/how-to-choose-a-rehab-program-as-a-family) lined up, rides arranged and one person responsible for following up with the treatment provider.

## How can our family put this into practice together?

The most helpful thing your family can do is decide, before the next crisis, how you'll respond together. A short routine keeps everyone on the same page and stops the person struggling from hearing five different messages.

1. **Agree on the facts.** In a calm family meeting, each person shares what they've actually seen: how much, how often and any withdrawal symptoms. No theories, just observations.

2. **Get a professional opinion.** Choose one person to call a doctor, a detox program or [an interventionist](/blog/working-with-an-interventionist-as-a-family) and report back. Let that answer settle the "Is detox necessary?" debate.

3. **Build one plan.** Write down the detox program, the next level of care and [how it will be paid for](/blog/how-to-pay-for-rehab-as-a-family). Keep a second option in case the first has no bed.

4. **Divide the roles.** One person handles calls with providers, one handles rides and logistics, one checks in with siblings or kids, and one simply stays with your loved one.

5. **Follow up together.** Set a check-in for discharge day and for one week later. Ask what's working and what needs adjusting, without blame.

## How do I take care of myself through this?

Watching someone you love go through withdrawal is frightening. Taking care of yourself keeps you steady enough to help.

- **Share the load.** You don't have to be the one who makes every call. That's what dividing roles is for.

- **Expect mixed feelings.** Relief, anger, guilt and hope can all show up in the same afternoon. That's normal.

- **Get your own support.** A family support group, counselor or trusted friend can give you a place to be honest.

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. If your loved one uses opioids, keep naloxone (Narcan) in the home and make sure everyone knows where it is. And if withdrawal, drinking or arguments about treatment ever turn into threats, intimidation or someone controlling money, phones or movement, your safety comes first. Step away and contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Medical Detox

### How long does medical detox take?

Most medical detox stays last three to seven days, depending on the substance, how much and how long it was used, and the person's health. The detox team adjusts the plan as symptoms change.

### Is medical detox the same as rehab?

No. Medical detox manages the physical withdrawal; rehab and ongoing treatment address the thinking, habits and relationships behind the addiction. Detox works best as the first step in a longer plan.

### Can we force someone into medical detox?

In most cases, adults have to agree to medical detox. Some states have involuntary commitment laws for serious situations, but they vary widely. An interventionist or local treatment professional can explain your options and help you invite your loved one in a way that makes "yes" more likely.

### What if they leave detox early?

It happens, and it doesn't mean all hope is lost. Stay calm, stick to the boundaries your family agreed on, and keep the door open: "We still have the plan, and we'll help you take the next step when you're ready." If they leave early from opioid detox, make sure naloxone is close by.

### Does insurance cover medical detox?

Many insurance plans cover medically necessary detox, but coverage and approval rules vary. Ask the detox program to verify benefits before admission, and assign one family member to track calls and paperwork.

## Detox is a doorway your family can walk through together

Medical detox isn't a sign that your loved one is hopeless, and it isn't a finish line. It's a safe doorway into recovery. When your family agrees on the facts, trusts a professional opinion and shares one plan for what comes next, that doorway leads somewhere.
