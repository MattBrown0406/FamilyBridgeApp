Your younger daughter brings home a great report card on the same night her brother doesn’t come home at all. You sign it at the kitchen counter, half listening, phone in your other hand. She says, “It’s fine,” and goes upstairs.

If you’ve ever wondered what life is like for the siblings of someone with addiction, start with that moment. When one child is in crisis, the family’s attention, worry and money flow toward them. The brothers and sisters often learn to need less, ask for less and say less. It doesn’t mean you love them less. It means you’re stretched past your limit.

This guide is for parents who want to protect their other children, and for siblings, young or grown, who feel forgotten. It covers what siblings go through, how parents can include and protect them, and how siblings can help without carrying it all.

## How does addiction affect siblings?

Siblings often feel invisible, angry, scared and guilty, sometimes all in the same week. Common experiences include:

- **Resentment:** the rules seem to apply only to them, while their sibling gets chance after chance.
- **Fear:** lying awake listening for the car, or dreading a phone call.
- **Guilt:** for being angry, for doing well, or for having fun while their sibling struggles.
- **Embarrassment:** not inviting friends over, or dodging questions at school.
- **Grief:** missing the brother or sister they used to know.

These are normal reactions to a hard situation, not signs of a bad attitude. Many siblings never say them out loud because they don’t want to add to their parents’ stress.

## Why do some siblings become the “good one”?

Many siblings decide, without ever saying it, that the family can’t handle one more problem, so they become the easy child. Straight A’s, no complaints, help around the house.

Parents feel relief, and it’s easy to stop checking in. But the “good one” is often carrying pressure, loneliness and worry nobody sees, and their own struggles — anxiety, depression, even their own drinking or drug use — can go unnoticed. Other siblings go the opposite way and act out, or pull away from the family completely. Two decades of family work have taught me that the sibling who seems “fine” is often the one carrying the most in silence.

## What happens when adult siblings are asked for money?

Adult siblings are often the quiet next stop once parents say no, and those requests can strain the relationship and undercut the family’s plan.

The text usually sounds casual: “Can you spot me a hundred till Friday? Don’t tell Mom.” Say no and you feel guilty; say yes and you become part of the cycle. Grown siblings may also resent watching parents drain savings on rescues, or worry about who will handle all this when their parents can’t.

The fairest answer is to [bring adult siblings into the family’s money decisions](/blog/should-i-give-my-addicted-son-money) so everyone gives the same response. A sibling can say: **“I’m not giving money, but I’m not going anywhere either. Want to grab lunch Saturday?”**

## How can parents include siblings without overloading them?

Tell siblings the truth at a level that fits their age, invite them into family conversations, and make it clear that fixing their brother or sister is not their job.

- **Explain honestly.** For a younger child: “Your brother has an illness called addiction. It’s not your fault, and you can’t fix it. The grown-ups are getting help.” Teenagers usually know more than you think, so offer more detail and invite questions.
- **Give them a voice.** Older children and adult siblings can join family meetings and say how the situation affects them.
- **Don’t make them the messenger, the spy or the babysitter.** And never ask them to keep secrets from the other parent.
- **If they share a worry, thank them and handle it yourself.** Don’t tell their sibling where the information came from.

## How can parents protect siblings at home?

Protect their safety, their space and their time with you.

- Lock up medications, alcohol, cash and valuables.
- Give each child a private space where their belongings won’t disappear.
- Schedule regular one-on-one time — even a weekly breakfast — where addiction isn’t the topic unless they bring it up.
- Show up for their games, recitals and milestones, even in the middle of a crisis.
- Watch for changes in sleep, grades, friends or mood, and consider a counselor of their own. Alateen, part of Al-Anon, offers meetings for teens.

If the child who is using threatens, hurts or steals from a brother or sister, everyone’s safety comes first. Don’t confront anyone without a safety plan; the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) can help you make one. If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

## How can siblings help without carrying it all?

If you’re the sibling reading this: you help most by staying a brother or sister, not a parent, counselor or bank.

- **Stay connected around normal life.** A movie, a game, a funny text. Time together when your sibling is sober matters more than you might think.
- **Be honest, kindly.** “I miss you. I’ve been worried since last weekend.” Concern lands better than a lecture.
- **Don’t cover, lie or lend money.** Backing up the family’s plan makes the message clear: everyone loves them, and everyone gives the same answer.
- **Get support of your own.** A counselor, a trusted friend, or a group like Nar-Anon or Al-Anon gives you a place to put feelings you may not want to pile onto your parents.
- **Let your own life move forward.** You’re allowed to graduate, travel, celebrate and be happy. Your sibling’s illness doesn’t put your life on hold.

## How can a family make room for everyone?

[Start with a short family meeting](/blog/how-to-run-a-family-meeting-about-addiction) where each child, young or grown, answers one question: “What’s one thing you need from us right now?” Then actually do one thing from each answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Siblings of Someone With Addiction

### I feel guilty that I’ve neglected my other kids. What can I do?

Most parents in this situation feel that way, and it isn’t too late. Start with one honest conversation: “I know a lot of my attention has gone to your brother. I’m sorry. I want to hear how you’re doing.” Then follow it with time, not just words.

### Are siblings at higher risk of substance problems themselves?

They can be, because addiction often runs in families through a mix of genes and environment. That’s not a prediction. It’s a reason to talk openly, watch for warning signs, and make sure they have support of their own.

### Should siblings be involved in treatment?

If they want to be, often yes. Many treatment programs welcome family members in family sessions. Ask the program what fits your child’s age, and let siblings choose how involved they want to be.

### What if a sibling wants nothing to do with their brother or sister?

Respect it. Distance can be self-protection, especially after years of hurt or broken promises. Don’t force a reunion. Let them know the door can stay open on their terms.
