Your son is in treatment. The intake call is over, and you hang up with a sinking feeling. He told them he drinks “a few beers on weekends.” You know about the bottles in the garage, the missed work and the night he couldn’t remember driving home.

If you’re wondering what to tell your loved one’s treatment team, you’re not alone. Many parents and spouses worry they’ll say too much, say too little, or make things worse. And when everyone in the family remembers things differently, it’s hard to know whose version to give.

This guide covers why your information matters, what to share, how to share it as one family instead of five separate voices, and how to stay steady when privacy rules mean the team can’t tell you much in return.

## Why does the treatment team need to hear from the family?

The treatment team needs to hear from you because you see the parts of the problem your loved one may not see, or may not say. Good care is built on an accurate picture, and families often hold key pieces of it.

People with a substance use problem often minimize their use. Usually that isn’t simple lying. Shame, fear and the way substances affect memory and judgment can all blur what a person reports about themselves.

In more than 20 years of working with families, the pattern I see most often is this: the family knows a great deal, but nobody has ever put it all in one place. One parent knows about the money. A sister knows about the late-night texts. A grandparent knows about the pills missing from the cabinet. The treatment team gets only fragments.

## What should we tell the treatment team?

Share clear, specific facts about your loved one’s substance use, health, safety and daily life, especially anything they might leave out. Facts help more than labels or opinions.

### Substance use and health

- What they use, how often and how much, as best you know
- Daily drinking or use of benzodiazepines or other sedatives, since withdrawal from these can be dangerous and needs medical care
- Any past overdoses, blackouts, seizures or hospital visits
- Past treatment, what helped and what didn’t
- Mental health concerns, diagnoses or medications you know about

### Safety

- Any talk of suicide or self-harm
- Threats, violence, weapons in the home or driving while impaired

### Daily life and family

- Changes in work, school, sleep, friendships or money
- Legal problems or debts
- What home looks like right now, including who lives there and who has been helping financially

Stick to what you’ve seen and heard. “He has been drinking every day since March, and he had a seizure last spring” helps the team far more than “He’s an alcoholic and he lies about everything.”

## What if the treatment center won’t tell us anything?

You can almost always give information to the treatment team, even when they can’t give any back. Privacy laws limit what staff can share with you, not what they can hear from you.

Substance use treatment records have strong federal privacy protections. If your loved one is an adult, staff usually need their written consent before telling you anything, and sometimes before confirming they’re a patient at all. Rules for minors can be different and vary by state, so ask the program directly.

It can feel like a wall. But you can still call, write a short summary and ask, “Can you make sure this gets to their therapist?” For more on consent and who does what, see our guide to [working with an interventionist as a family](/blog/working-with-an-interventionist-as-a-family).

## How do we share information as one family instead of five different stories?

Gather what everyone knows, agree on one honest summary, and choose one person to share it. When five relatives call with five different versions, the team gets confused and your loved one may play one account against another.

### Hold a family meeting first

Sit down before anyone calls. Ask each person, “What have you seen that the treatment team should know?” Write it all down, without arguing about who is right.

### Agree on the facts, not the blame

You don’t have to agree on why this happened. You only need to agree on what happened. If two people remember something differently, say so plainly in the summary.

### Pick a point person

Choose one calm, organized person to be the main contact. Others can still share later, but one steady voice keeps information from getting lost or mixed up.

### Include the hard parts about the family

Be honest about patterns at home, too: money that was handed over, problems that were covered up, rules nobody held. That isn’t confessing failure. It helps the team plan for what happens when your loved one comes home. Our article on [family secrets and addiction](/blog/family-secrets-and-addiction) can help if this part feels hard, and the [“Am I enabling?” exercise](/enabling-exercise) is a gentle way to start.

## Should we tell our loved one what we shared?

Usually, yes. Being open about it builds trust better than working behind their back. You don’t need their permission to share what you’ve seen, but secrecy tends to backfire later.

Ask the team how they handle what families share. Some may bring it into your loved one’s sessions. If you have a safety concern, such as threats, ask the team how to share it in a way that keeps everyone safe.

When you talk to your loved one, keep it simple and warm:

- “We told your counselor what we’ve seen, because we want you to get the best care possible.”
- “We’re not trying to get you in trouble. We’re trying to be on your side.”

## How can our family put this into practice together?

Turn the advice into a simple routine: gather, agree, share and follow up. Doing it together keeps one person from carrying it all.

1. Add “What should the treatment team know?” to your next family meeting agenda.
2. Write one shared summary in plain facts.
3. Agree on your point person and how others pass along new information.
4. Decide which updates you will share with your loved one, and how.
5. Check in again before discharge, when plans for coming home take shape.

## How do I take care of myself through this?

Get your own support, separate from your loved one’s treatment. Handing your story to strangers can stir up grief, anger and guilt, and you deserve a place to set that down.

Family support groups like Al-Anon and Nar-Anon can help, and you can find one through our [meeting finder](/meetings). Many treatment centers also offer family education or support. Sleep, eat and spend time with people who don’t need anything from you.

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, is free, confidential and open 24/7. Naloxone (Narcan) is available without a prescription at most U.S. pharmacies.

If someone threatens, hurts or controls you, your safety comes first. Don’t confront anyone or issue an ultimatum without a safety plan. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233.

## Frequently Asked Questions About What to Tell Your Loved One’s Treatment Team

### Will sharing information get my loved one kicked out of treatment?

It is unlikely that honest information alone would get someone discharged. Treatment teams expect families to share concerns, and accurate details help them choose the right care. If you’re worried, ask the program how they use family information.

### What if my family disagrees about what happened?

Write down what you agree on, and note where you disagree. The team can handle different views. What confuses them is contradictory calls from several relatives who never compared notes.

### Can I share information if my loved one hasn’t signed a release?

Usually, yes. Privacy rules mainly limit what staff can tell you. They may not be able to confirm your loved one is there, but you can still ask them to pass your information to the right person.

### Should I share things my loved one told me in confidence?

If it involves safety, such as suicide, overdose, violence or dangerous withdrawal, share it. For other private matters, think about whether the team truly needs it, and consider telling your loved one you plan to share it.

## You’re Part of the Picture

Your loved one’s treatment team sees them for a short time. Your family has seen the whole story. When you gather what you know, agree on the facts and speak with one calm voice, you give the team a clearer picture, and you give your loved one a family that’s pulling in the same direction.

You don’t have to get it perfect. You just have to do it together.
