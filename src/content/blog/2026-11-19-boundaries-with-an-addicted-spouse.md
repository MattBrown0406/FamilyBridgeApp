It’s 6:15 on a Thursday evening. Dinner is on the table, the kids are asking where Dad is, and you already know what his voice will sound like when he finally walks in. Your teenager won’t look at you. You’ve said “this is the last time” so often that nobody in the house believes it anymore, including you.

Setting boundaries with an addicted spouse is different from setting them with an adult child or a sibling. You share a bed, a bank account, a mortgage and maybe children. You can’t simply ask them to move out without turning the whole household upside down. And because the marriage sits at the center of the family, every boundary you set, or don’t set, ripples out to kids, in-laws and friends.

This guide is for the spouse in the middle and for everyone around you: adult children, parents, siblings and in-laws. We’ll cover what a boundary with a spouse actually is, which ones matter most, how to hold them without daily fights, how to bring the rest of the family onto the same plan and how to take care of yourself through it.

## What does a boundary with an addicted spouse actually look like?

A boundary with an addicted spouse is a clear statement about what *you* will do when a specific behavior happens. It is not a rule you enforce on your partner, and it is not a threat you hope will scare them sober. The best boundaries protect you, your children and your finances, whether or not your spouse changes.

### A boundary describes your action, not theirs

“You need to stop drinking” is a request. “If you’ve been drinking, I won’t ride in the car with you, and I’ll drive the kids myself” is a boundary. The first depends on your spouse’s choices. The second depends only on yours.

### Boundaries vs. ultimatums

An ultimatum usually comes out in anger, sounds like a punishment and gets dropped the next morning. A boundary is calm, planned ahead of time and repeated as often as needed. In more than 20 years of working with families, I’ve seen that the spouse who says less, and follows through more, gets taken seriously far sooner. For more on the difference, see [boundaries vs. ultimatums](/blog/boundaries-vs-ultimatums-addiction).

## Which boundaries matter most when you’re married to someone with addiction?

Start with safety, children and money, in that order. Those three areas carry the highest risk and give you the most protection for your effort.

Common, workable boundaries with an addicted spouse include:
- **Driving:** “If you’ve been using, you don’t drive the kids. I will, or we’ll call someone.”
- **Children:** “I won’t leave the kids alone with you if you’ve been drinking or using.”
- **Money:** “I’m moving my paycheck into an account in my name to cover the mortgage and groceries.”
- **Conversations:** “I won’t discuss your use while you’re intoxicated. We’ll talk tomorrow.”
- **Cover stories:** “I won’t call your boss or make excuses to your family anymore.”
- **Substances in the house:** “Alcohol and drugs aren’t kept in our home.”

Pick two or three to start. A short list you actually hold beats a long list that collapses by Sunday.

## How do I hold a boundary without it turning into a fight?

Say it once, calmly, when your spouse is sober, then act on it without debate. Arguing in the moment gives the addiction a chance to negotiate. Your actions will communicate more than any speech.

### Use a calm, sober-moment conversation

Choose a quiet time, not right after an incident. Lead with care: “I love you, and I want our family to be okay. Here’s what I’m going to do from now on.” This is the heart of CRAFT, an evidence-based approach that teaches families to reward sober behavior and step back from use-related chaos rather than lecturing or pleading.

### Expect pushback, and don’t argue with it

Your spouse may get angry or test the boundary within days. That isn’t failure; it’s proof they noticed. Repeat one short sentence (“I’m not going to argue about it, I’m going to do what I said”) and follow through.

### Reward the good days

When your spouse is sober and present, notice it and plan time together. Boundaries work best alongside warmth, not instead of it.

## How do we get the rest of the family on the same page about boundaries with an addicted spouse?

The rest of the family needs to know your boundaries and agree not to undercut them. If you stop covering for your spouse but your mother-in-law starts, the boundary disappears. A short, honest family conversation turns one tired spouse into a team, and it’s the first step to learning how to [set boundaries as a family](/blog/boundaries-as-a-family-addicted-loved-one).

### Who needs to be in the loop
- **Adult children and older teens:** They already know more than you think. Tell them the plan in age-appropriate terms and that none of this is their fault or their job to fix.
- **In-laws:** They may defend their son or daughter. Ask for one thing only: “Please don’t give money or make excuses. Point them back to us and to help.”

### Agree on what each person will and won’t do

When someone gets the 2 a.m. call asking for cash or a ride, everyone should already know the answer. Families who share one plan stop getting played against each other.

## How can our family put this into practice together?

A simple weekly routine keeps boundaries from fading. The goal is consistency and shared follow-through, not perfection:
1. **Hold a 20-minute family check-in each week.** Include the spouse in the middle plus one or two trusted relatives. Keep it calm and short.
2. **Review the facts, not the feelings first.** What happened this week? Were any boundaries tested? Stick to what was seen and heard.
3. **Confirm the current boundaries.** Read them out loud. Adjust only if something clearly isn’t working, not because someone felt guilty.
4. **Assign roles.** Who drives the kids if needed? Who handles calls from the spouse’s family? Who checks in on the spouse in the middle?
5. **Plan one positive moment.** Choose an activity to share with your spouse when they’re sober, so they see what recovery could feel like.
6. **Set one follow-up.** Call a treatment program, book a family therapy appointment or attend a support meeting before next week.

## What if my spouse refuses help or crosses the line anyway?

If your spouse crosses a boundary, you follow through with the action you named, without extra punishment. If nothing changes, you may need professional help to plan a next step.

### When it’s time to bring in a professional

If months of steady boundaries haven’t shifted anything, talk with an addiction counselor, a family therapist or a certified interventionist. An interventionist is a trained professional who helps families plan a structured, loving conversation that invites the person into treatment.

### Your safety comes first

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

Boundaries are for ordinary hard situations, not for abuse. If your spouse threatens you, controls your money or movements, damages property to intimidate you or becomes physically violent, your priority is safety, not a boundary conversation. The National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788) can help you make a safety plan confidentially.

## How do I take care of myself through this?

Taking care of yourself isn’t selfish; it’s what keeps you steady enough to hold the line. Spouses of people with addiction carry enormous stress, often in secret. You need support that belongs to you, not just to your partner’s recovery.
- **Find your own meeting.** Al-Anon, Nar-Anon and SMART Recovery Family & Friends are full of people who understand without explanation.
- **Tell one trusted person the truth.** Secrecy is exhausting. One honest friend can lighten the load.
- **Consider your own counselor.** Someone who is in your corner alone can help you sort out what you want, not just what you can tolerate.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Boundaries With an Addicted Spouse

### Is setting boundaries with an addicted spouse the same as threatening divorce?

No. A boundary describes what you’ll do about a specific behavior, such as not riding in the car after drinking. Divorce is a much larger decision that you can make, or not make, on your own timeline.

### What if my spouse says my boundaries are controlling?

Boundaries govern your actions, not your spouse’s. You’re choosing where your money goes, who drives your children and which conversations you join. Your spouse still makes their own choices; you just stop absorbing the consequences.

### Should I tell the kids about the boundaries?

Yes, in age-appropriate terms. Kids feel safer when they know the plan, for example, “If Dad has been drinking, Mom drives.” Make sure they hear that the addiction is not their fault and not their job to fix.

### How long before boundaries make a difference?

Some spouses notice changes within weeks; for others it takes months. What matters most is consistency, because a boundary held three times out of four teaches your spouse that it’s negotiable. Keep holding it, keep getting support and stay open to professional help.

## You don’t have to hold the line alone

Living with an addicted spouse can make you feel trapped between leaving and losing yourself. There is a third path: clear boundaries, a family that backs you up and steady support for your own wellbeing. Boundaries with an addicted spouse aren’t about giving up on your marriage. They’re about giving it, and your family, a real chance.
