It's 2:40 a.m. and you hear the back door. You lie still, listening to footsteps, a cabinet, the fridge. In the morning there's an empty bottle in the recycling that nobody will claim, your car has a quarter tank less gas, and your husband is already saying, "We need to talk about this." Your daughter texts from college: "Is he still living there?" Everyone has an opinion about your adult child with addiction living at home, and none of the opinions match.

When a grown son or daughter is drinking or using under your roof, home stops feeling like home. One parent wants him out by Friday; the other is afraid of what happens if he leaves. Meanwhile, the person struggling learns which door to knock on for a yes.

This guide is for the whole family: parents, spouses, siblings, adult children and grandparents. We'll cover how to decide whether staying is workable, how to write simple house rules together, what to do when a rule is broken, and how to keep everyone safe. The goal isn't a perfect house. It's one plan that everyone can stand behind.

## Should our adult child with addiction keep living at home?

There is no single right answer. Living at home can be a stable base for recovery, or it can make continued use comfortable. The deciding question is usually whether your family can agree on clear conditions and actually hold them.

In more than 20 years of working with families, I've seen both outcomes. The homes that improved weren't the strictest or the softest. They were the ones where the adults said the same thing and followed through the same way.

### Questions to answer together first

Before anyone decides "stay" or "go," sit down and answer these honestly:

- Is everyone in the house physically safe right now?
- Are there younger children or vulnerable adults living here?
- Is our loved one willing to take any step toward help, even a small one?
- Can both parents (or all decision-makers) agree on conditions?
- What will we do if the conditions aren't met?

If you can't answer the last question yet, the family needs a meeting before the conversation with your loved one.

## What house rules make sense when an adult child is using?

Good house rules are few, specific, and connected to safety and recovery rather than punishment. Three to five rules are plenty to start. Each one should be something your family can see, measure and enforce without a fight.

Skip vague rules like "respect this house." Clear rules sound like this:

- No alcohol or drugs kept or used in the house or the car.
- Home by an agreed time, or a text if plans change.
- No guests overnight without asking first.
- Contributes something each week: rent, chores, or hours at a job or program.
- Takes one recovery step: an assessment, a counselor, a meeting, or treatment.

### Write it down as a living agreement

A one-page "living-at-home agreement" in plain language keeps everyone honest, parents included. When memories differ later, the page settles it, not whoever argues loudest.

Consider adding what the parents agree to do, too: no yelling, no bringing up old history at dinner. Rules that go both ways feel fair. Our guide to [setting boundaries as a family](/blog/setting-boundaries-as-a-family) can help you put those limits into words.

## How do we get every family member to agree on the rules?

Agree among yourselves first, then present the rules together. If one parent reveals the rules while the other stays silent, your loved one hears a split, not a plan. Unity matters more than the exact wording of any single rule.

[Hold a family meeting](/blog/how-to-run-a-family-meeting-about-addiction) without your loved one present. Include anyone who gives money or rides, because a rule only works if nobody quietly undoes it. A grandparent who sends $200 "for groceries" can cancel out a rent rule without meaning to.

### When one person won't go along

Disagreement is normal; often one parent is afraid and the other is exhausted. Look for the smallest rule everyone can hold, start there, and agree to revisit it in two weeks. A modest rule held by everyone beats a strong rule held by one.

## What do we do when a house rule gets broken?

Respond the way you agreed in advance, calmly and without a speech. The consequence should already be written in the agreement, so the conversation is about following the plan, not inventing a punishment in the moment.

This is where most families get stuck. Someone either threatens something huge or lets it slide. Both teach your loved one the rules aren't real. Understanding [boundaries vs. ultimatums](/blog/boundaries-vs-ultimatums-addiction) helps keep the response grounded in what your family will actually do.

### Keep consequences small and predictable

Consequences work best when they're proportionate and connected to the rule:

1. Missed curfew without a text: no car use the next day.
2. Alcohol found in the house: it's poured out, and the family meeting moves up to this week.
3. Weekly contribution skipped: no cash or extras until it's caught up.
4. Repeated violations: the conversation shifts to treatment or a different living arrangement.

Say it once, kindly: "We agreed that if there was alcohol in the house, we'd meet sooner. So we're meeting Thursday." Then stop talking. You don't need to win the argument, only do what you said.

## How do we talk to our loved one without starting a fight?

Pick a calm, sober moment, keep it short, and lead with care before the rules. Talking during a crisis almost always turns into a fight.

Communication approaches like CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training, a research-backed method that teaches families to encourage change through positive communication) suggest using "I" statements, naming specific behavior, and offering help in the same breath. For example: "We love you and we want you here. We're also scared. These are the things we need for you to stay, and we'll help you find support if you want it."

Then listen. If your loved one pushes back or storms off, that isn't failure. It's the first round, and the offer of help stays open.

## How can our family put this into practice together?

Turn the agreement into a simple routine so it doesn't depend on anyone's mood. A short, regular rhythm keeps the family coordinated and small problems small.

1. **Hold a family-only meeting.** Agree on three to five house rules and what happens if each one is broken.
2. **Write the agreement and assign roles.** Decide who presents it, who tracks contributions, and who handles curfew texts.
3. **Present it together.** Every decision-maker in the room, same words, same tone.
4. **Check in weekly.** Fifteen minutes on the same day each week: what went well, what was broken, what we did about it.
5. **Follow through as a team.** Whoever is on duty applies the agreed consequence, and everyone else backs them up.
6. **Revisit every month.** Loosen rules that are working, tighten ones that aren't, and keep treatment options on the table.

## How do I take care of myself through this?

You can't hold a boundary well if you're running on four hours of sleep and constant dread. Taking care of yourself isn't selfish. It's part of the plan.

- Protect your sleep, even if that means a lock on your bedroom door.
- Find a family support group, such as Al-Anon, Nar-Anon, or a SMART Recovery Family & Friends meeting.
- Keep one part of your week that has nothing to do with addiction.
- Let siblings step back when they need to. Not everyone carries the same load.

Guilt makes rules wobble. Support makes them steady.

### Safety comes first

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. If your loved one becomes threatening, controlling or violent, your safety comes before any house rule. You can contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 for confidential help making a safety plan, and you don't have to wait for things to get worse to reach out.

## Frequently Asked Questions About an Adult Child With Addiction Living at Home

### Is it enabling to let my adult child live at home?

Not automatically. Living at home becomes enabling when it shields your loved one from the natural results of their use with no expectations attached. A home with clear rules, follow-through, and an open door to treatment is support, not enabling.

### Can we make our adult child move out?

Yes, in most cases, but the process depends on where you live. In some states an adult who has lived in the home for a while may have tenant rights, so a family may need to follow a formal notice process. Check with a local legal aid office before you set a move-out date. (I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice.)

### What if our adult child refuses to follow any rules?

Then the family's job is to stay united and keep doing what you said you would. Refusal is often the start of a negotiation, not the end. This is also a good time to talk with a counselor or interventionist about next steps.

### What if one parent keeps breaking the agreement?

Bring it back to the family meeting, not a fight in the hallway. Ask what made the rule hard to hold and adjust it. The goal is a plan both parents can keep.

## One Home, One Plan

Having an adult child with addiction living at home is one of the hardest things a family can face. You don't have to choose between love and limits. When your family agrees on a few clear rules, says them together, and follows through calmly, home can become a steadier place, for everyone, including the person who is struggling.

Start small. Hold one family meeting this week. Write down three rules. Decide who does what. Then keep showing up, together.
