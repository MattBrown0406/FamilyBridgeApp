You told your daughter she couldn’t come over high. So she went to her aunt’s, and her aunt let her sleep on the couch. You stopped paying her car insurance, and her grandfather quietly paid it instead. Every boundary you set seems to have a back door, and you’re the one who ends up looking like the bad guy.

Learning how to set boundaries as a family with an addicted loved one is different from setting them on your own. A boundary only one person holds is easy to get around. A boundary the whole family holds, calmly and with love, becomes something your loved one can actually count on.

This guide covers how to create boundaries together, hold them consistently and keep love at the center of each one.

## What is a family boundary?

A family boundary is a clear statement of what the family will do if a certain thing happens. It’s about your own actions, not about controlling your loved one’s.

“You can’t drink anymore” is a rule you can’t enforce. “If you’ve been drinking, we’ll end the visit and see you another day” is a boundary, because you can follow through no matter what anyone else does.

Boundaries also differ from threats and punishments. A threat is said in anger and rarely followed. A punishment is meant to make someone suffer. A boundary protects your home, your health and your values, and it’s stated calmly, ahead of time.

## Why do boundaries work better when the whole family agrees?

Because your loved one experiences the family as a whole. When every door gives the same answer, a boundary becomes predictable and believable.

When pushing works some of the time, people learn to push harder. So a boundary held by Mom but not by Grandpa doesn’t just fail at Grandpa’s house; it teaches your loved one to keep testing everywhere.

Agreeing together also spreads the weight. No one has to be the villain, because the decision belongs to everyone. In my years as an interventionist, the families who make the most progress usually aren’t the strictest ones. They’re the most consistent.

## How do we set boundaries together, step by step?

Start with the situations that hurt most, write each boundary as an if-then about your own actions, make sure every adult can follow through, and decide who does what.

### 1. List what keeps happening

Money requests, showing up intoxicated, using at home, driving after drinking, yelling. Get it all on paper.

### 2. Choose two or three to start

Not twenty. A few boundaries you hold every time will do more than a long list no one remembers.

### 3. Write each one as an if-then

- “If you’re under the influence when you visit, we’ll end the visit and try again another day.”
- “If you’ve been drinking, you won’t drive our car. We’ll give you a ride or pay for one.”
- “If the conversation turns to yelling or name-calling, we’ll stop and pick it up tomorrow.”
- “If you use in our home, you’ll need to make other living arrangements, and we’ll help you look at treatment or sober living.”

### 4. Check that everyone can hold it

If Grandpa truly can’t turn your loved one away at his door, don’t put him on the front line. Give him a different role, like calling you right away. A boundary someone can’t hold isn’t a boundary yet.

### 5. Decide who does what

Who will say it? Who will follow through? Who will call to support that person afterward?

### 6. Make sure it’s safe

Never set a boundary that requires someone who drinks heavily or takes benzodiazepines or other sedatives to stop suddenly on their own. Withdrawal from these can be dangerous. A safer version is “We’ll help you see a doctor to stop safely,” and a doctor is the right person for medical questions. Keeping naloxone (Narcan) at home isn’t approving of drug use; it’s a safety step, and it’s available without a prescription at most U.S. pharmacies.

## How do we tell our loved one about the boundaries?

Calmly, at a sober moment, briefly and with love up front. Never as a surprise confrontation.

Have one or two people deliver it, not the whole family. You might say:

“We love you and we want you in our lives. As a family, we’ve agreed on a few things we’re going to do differently. If you come over after using, we’ll ask you to leave and come back another day. That isn’t because we’re angry. It’s because we want to spend time with the real you.”

You don’t need to debate it. If your loved one argues, try: “We’ve decided this together. We’ll look at it again in a month.”

## How do we hold boundaries consistently when it gets hard?

Expect to be tested, follow through calmly every time, and back each other up, especially right after someone holds the line.

- **Expect it to get louder before it gets better.** Testing is normal when old patterns stop working.
- **Let the action be the message.** No lectures, no “I told you so.” Just do what you said.
- **Allow natural consequences.** When using leads to a missed shift or a ruined weekend, don’t rush to fix it. That’s different from adding punishments.
- **If someone caves, reset without blame.** “We didn’t hold this last week. We’re starting again today.”
- **Support whoever held it.** A quick text like “That was hard. You did it. We’re with you” keeps people going.

If holding a boundary leads to threats, violence or someone trying to control you, your safety comes first. Don’t deliver ultimatums without a safety plan; the National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233, can help. If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

## How do we keep love in our boundaries?

Pair every boundary with something you’ll gladly do, and say yes generously when your loved one is sober or taking steps toward help.

The “no” gets all the attention, but the “yes” does much of the work. Approaches like CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training) teach families to make sober time warm and rewarding: a dinner invitation, a movie, real interest in their day. That gives your loved one something to move toward, not just something to avoid.

- “We won’t pay your rent, and we will help pay for an assessment the day you’re ready.”
- “We won’t visit when you’ve been using, and we’d love to have you for Sunday lunch when you haven’t.”

## Can boundaries protect my own well-being too?

Yes. Some of the most important boundaries are about you: when you’ll take non-emergency calls, how much you can afford to give, and what you need to stay healthy.

Protecting your sleep, your job and your other relationships isn’t abandoning your loved one. It’s how you stay steady enough to help for the long haul. Al-Anon and Nar-Anon can help you hold that line, and SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, offers free, confidential treatment information and referrals 24/7.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Setting Boundaries as a Family

### What if one family member won’t agree to a boundary?

Don’t force it. Look for a smaller version they can hold, or give them a different role. A boundary everyone keeps beats a bigger one only half the family holds.

### Isn’t this just tough love?

No. “Tough love” often means cutting someone off or waiting for them to hit rock bottom. Family boundaries protect the family while keeping the relationship, and the invitation to get help, open.

### Should our boundaries change over time?

Yes. Review them regularly, for example at [a weekly family meeting](/blog/how-to-run-a-family-meeting-about-addiction). As your loved one [rebuilds trust in recovery](/blog/rebuilding-trust-after-addiction-as-a-family), some boundaries can loosen step by step.

### What if our loved one says our boundaries will make them use more?

Acknowledge the feeling without taking responsibility for their use: “I hear that you’re hurting. We love you, and this is still our decision.” Keep safety steps in place, and consider talking with an interventionist or counselor.

## Boundaries Held Together, With Love

A family boundary isn’t a wall. It’s more like a guardrail: steady, predictable, and there to keep everyone safer, including the person you’re worried about.
