It's 11 p.m. and your son's car finally pulls into the driveway, an hour later than he said. He comes in, says hi a little too fast, and heads straight for the shower. Nothing you could point to. But your stomach knows. You text your wife upstairs: "Did he seem off to you?" She writes back: "Please don't start."

When you suspect a loved one is using again, the not-knowing can feel worse than bad news. One person sees warning signs everywhere. Another sees nothing at all. Someone else is quietly checking bank statements and telling no one. Before long, the family is arguing about *whether* there's a problem instead of deciding what to do about it.

This guide covers how to tell a hunch from a pattern, how to compare notes without becoming a surveillance team, how to bring it up with your loved one, and what to do whether the answer is yes, no, or "I don't know." The goal: one calm, shared response instead of five worried, separate ones.

## What should we do first if we suspect a loved one is using again?

Slow down and write down what you've actually seen. Before anyone confronts, accuses, or searches a room, get the facts out of your head and onto paper, then share them with the rest of the family.

Suspicion feeds on itself. Once you start watching, everything looks like evidence: a closed door, a tired face, a phone turned face-down. Writing things down separates what happened from what you fear it means.

### Observations vs. conclusions

- **Observation:** "He missed work Tuesday and Thursday." **Conclusion:** "He's using again."
- **Observation:** "$200 left her account that she can't explain." **Conclusion:** "She's buying drugs."

You can say an observation out loud without starting a fight. A conclusion might turn out to be right, but it's not where the conversation should start.

## What are the early signs a loved one may be using again?

Early signs usually show up as changes in patterns, not one dramatic moment. Missed commitments, new secrecy, money that doesn't add up, recovery routines falling away, and old friends or hangouts returning are the most common.

Watch for clusters of changes over a few weeks, such as:

- Skipping support meetings, therapy, or check-ins "just this week"
- Defensiveness or irritability when routine questions come up
- Sleep that's suddenly off: up all night, or sleeping through the day
- Money problems, missing cash, or vague explanations for purchases
- Reconnecting with people or places tied to past use

In more than 20 years of working with families, I've learned that the first sign is rarely the substance itself. It's the recovery routine quietly slipping: the meeting skipped once, then twice, the sponsor calls that stop. That's the moment to pay attention, not to panic.

Stress, depression, a new job, or a breakup can look similar. No single change proves anything. A pattern is a reason to talk, not a verdict.

## How do we compare notes as a family without becoming detectives?

Agree on one place where everyone shares observations, and one person who keeps the overall picture. Share facts, not theories, and decide together what you won't do, such as secret phone searches or ambush-style interrogations.

When five family members each hold one piece of the puzzle, nobody sees the whole pattern, or one parent carries it alone, unsure whether they're overreacting. Pooling observations fixes both problems and makes it harder for anyone to be played against each other.

### Ground rules for comparing notes

- Share what you saw or heard, with dates, not what you think it means.
- No "gotcha" moments. Nobody confronts alone with a pile of evidence.
- Agree in advance on limits, like no reading their texts or tracking their phone without their knowledge.
- If drug testing was already part of an agreed recovery plan, follow that plan. Don't spring new tests as a trap.

## How do we talk to our loved one when we suspect they're using again?

Choose one or two people with the calmest relationship, pick a sober, private moment, and lead with care and specific observations rather than accusations. You're aiming for an honest conversation, not a confession.

### What to say

Try something like: "I've noticed you've missed your Tuesday meeting the last couple of weeks, and you've seemed really worn out. I love you and I'm worried. How are you doing, honestly?"

That opening names what you've seen, shows you care, and leaves room to answer. "Are you using again? Don't lie to me" almost guarantees a denial and an argument.

This draws on [CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training)](/blog/craft-method-for-families), an evidence-based approach that teaches families to communicate in ways that make honesty and recovery more rewarding than hiding.

### What if they deny it?

Don't argue about the facts. You can say, "Okay. I hope that's true. If something changes, we'd always rather know, and we're here to help." Then calmly restate the plan your family already agreed on.

A denial isn't the end of the conversation. Keep noting patterns as a family, and revisit it if things don't improve.

## What if our loved one admits they've been using again?

Thank them for telling the truth, stay as calm as you can, and turn to the plan. Honesty is the behavior you want more of, and the way your family reacts in the first five minutes shapes whether they'll be honest next time.

A slip — a brief return to use — is different from a full return to old patterns, but both are worth taking seriously. Reach out to their counselor, [treatment team](/blog/what-to-tell-your-loved-ones-treatment-team), or sponsor, ideally with your loved one involved. If your family already has a [relapse prevention plan](/blog/family-relapse-prevention-plan), now is the time to use it. If you don't, make one together this week.

After a period of not using, tolerance drops, so using the old amount can lead to overdose, especially with opioids. Many families keep naloxone (Narcan), the over-the-counter medicine that reverses opioid overdoses, at home.

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. And if past confrontations have led to threats, intimidation, or violence, don't have this conversation alone. Put your safety first, involve a professional, and know that the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) is available around the clock.

## How can our family put this into practice together?

Treat the suspicion as a shared family concern, not one person's private worry. A simple routine keeps everyone calm and moving in the same direction.

1. **Write it down.** Each family member notes specific observations, with dates, for one week.
2. **Meet briefly.** Hold a 30-minute family meeting to compare notes. Facts first, feelings second, conclusions last.
3. **Decide who talks and when.** Choose one or two people to have the conversation and agree on the opening words together.
4. **Agree on next steps for every answer.** Plan what you'll do if they say yes, no, or won't talk, so nobody improvises under stress.
5. **Follow up in one week.** Meet again to share how things are going and adjust the plan.

## How do I take care of myself through this?

Recognize that constant watching wears you down, and you can't be your loved one's 24-hour monitor. Share the load with your family, protect your sleep, and get support of your own.

Living in suspicion keeps you on high alert, checking their eyes, their tone, the time they get home. That vigilance makes sense, but it can't be sustained, and it rarely changes what your loved one does.

- Take turns being the "point person" so no one carries it every day.
- Set a time limit on worry talk, then do something ordinary together.
- Attend Al-Anon, Nar-Anon, or SMART Recovery Family & Friends.

## Frequently Asked Questions About When You Suspect a Loved One Is Using Again

### Should we drug test a loved one we suspect is using again?

Testing works best when it's part of an agreed recovery plan, ideally set up with a treatment provider. Surprise tests used as a trap tend to damage trust and lead to more hiding. If testing feels necessary, discuss it as a family first, then talk openly with your loved one about it.

### Is it okay to search their room or phone?

With adults, secret searches usually backfire, eroding trust and turning the relationship into hide-and-seek. The exception is immediate safety: if you believe someone is at risk of overdose or harm, safety comes first.

### What if I'm the only one in the family who thinks something is wrong?

Share specific observations, not your conclusion, and ask others to watch for a week. Often relatives have noticed things but stayed quiet. If they still disagree, you can talk with your loved one calmly yourself and get support for your own worry.

### What's the difference between a slip and a relapse?

A slip is usually brief use followed by a quick return to recovery. A relapse is a return to old patterns of use. Both deserve a calm response, and neither erases the progress that came before.

### Should we tell our loved one's sponsor or therapist?

Encourage your loved one to tell them directly, or reach out together. Privacy laws may limit what a provider can share with you, but you can always share your concerns.

## You Don't Have to Carry the Question Alone

That knot in your stomach deserves attention, not dismissal. A hunch handled alone often turns into a fight. A hunch handled as a family can become an honest conversation and, if needed, a quick return to help.

Start small. Write down what you've seen this week, ask your family to do the same, then sit down together and choose one calm next step.
