The first night after your daughter checks in, the house is strangely quiet. No phone buzzing at 1 a.m. No listening for the car in the driveway. Part of you is relieved. Another part has no idea what to do with all that worry now that it has nowhere to go.

Many families spend rehab simply waiting. But knowing what families should do while a loved one is in rehab can shape what happens when they come home. The treatment team is working with your loved one. These weeks are your family’s chance to do its own work.

This guide covers your family’s role, the treatment center’s family program and your own support, getting on the same page before they come home, money and visits, preparing for aftercare, and taking care of yourselves.

## What is the family’s role while a loved one is in treatment?

Your role is to heal, learn and get organized, not to run their treatment. The clinical team handles their care. Your family’s job is to change the home they’ll come back to.

Addiction reshapes a whole family. People start rescuing, keeping secrets, walking on eggshells or quietly taking sides. If your loved one changes in treatment and the family doesn’t, they come home to the same old system. In my experience, families who use this time for their own work tend to have a steadier homecoming than families who spend it waiting by the phone.

Respect their privacy, too. Treatment programs generally need your loved one’s written permission before they can share details with you. If you want updates, ask your loved one to sign a release, and try not to take it personally if information is limited.

## Should we join the treatment center’s family program or get our own support?

Do both if you can. The treatment center’s family program helps you understand what your loved one is working on, and support of your own helps with what you’ve been carrying.

- **The center’s family program.** Many treatment centers offer family education, family sessions or a family weekend, in person or online. Ask what’s available, and go even if it feels uncomfortable.
- **Family sessions with your loved one.** These can be hard, honest conversations with a counselor in the room. That’s the point. It’s a safer place to say difficult things than your kitchen table.
- **Support just for you.** A counselor of your own, or groups like Al-Anon and Nar-Anon, give you somewhere to put the fear and anger. Brothers, sisters and children in the home often need support too.

## How do we get on the same page before they come home?

Sit down as a family, without your loved one, and agree on the basics now while things are calm. Decisions made in a quiet house hold up far better than decisions made at the front door.

Talk through:

- If they’ll live with you, what the house expectations will be: sobriety, recovery meetings, work or school, curfew, chores.
- [What will happen if those expectations aren’t met](/blog/boundaries-as-a-family-addicted-loved-one), and who will say it.
- Whether to remove alcohol from the house and lock up prescription medications.
- Who will be the main contact with the treatment team.
- What you’ll tell relatives and friends, and what stays private.

Write your answers down. Then share them with your loved one, ideally in a family session with their counselor, so the plan feels like a starting point you build together rather than a list of rules sprung on them at the door.

## How should we handle money and visits during rehab?

Follow the program’s rules for visits and calls, and don’t send cash. Money and contact are two places where families can accidentally undo progress.

### Visits and calls

Many programs limit contact during the first days or weeks. Respect that; it helps your loved one settle in. When you do visit, keep it warm and save heavy topics for family sessions.

Expect at least one call where your loved one wants to leave or says the program is terrible. That’s common. Stay supportive without offering a ride home: “I hear how hard this is. Please talk to your counselor about it. I’m proud of you for being there.”

### Money

Pay the program and any approved costs directly, and buy needed items yourself instead of sending cash. Agree as a family on who handles money while they’re away, including their phone, car payment or rent, so no one can be approached alone.

## How do we prepare for aftercare now?

Start planning the next step while your loved one is still in treatment, with the treatment team’s help. Discharge day shouldn’t be the first time anyone asks what comes next.

Questions to ask the team:

- What level of care do you recommend after discharge, such as outpatient treatment or sober living?
- If they’ll live at home, what support do they need there?
- Will therapy, recovery meetings and medical appointments be booked before they leave?
- [What warning signs should we watch for](/blog/family-relapse-prevention-plan), and what should we do if we see them?

Talk about overdose risk, too. After a stretch without using, tolerance drops, so going back to an old amount can be deadly. If opioids have been part of the picture, keep naloxone (Narcan) at home. It’s available without a prescription at most U.S. pharmacies.

## How do we take care of ourselves while they’re in treatment?

Rest, and don’t feel guilty about it. You may have spent months or years on high alert, and your body and mind need time to come down.

- Sleep, eat real meals and see the friends you’ve been canceling on.
- Give attention to the other people in the house, especially kids who have been in the background.
- Notice the feelings that show up now that it’s quiet, including anger and grief. They’re normal.

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, offers free, confidential support 24/7.

## Frequently Asked Questions About What Families Should Do During Rehab

### Can we share information with the treatment team?

Usually, yes. Even without a signed release, you can typically give the team useful background, such as your loved one’s history, what you’ve seen at home and your biggest concerns. The team may not be able to respond with details, but what you share can still help.

### What if our loved one doesn’t want us involved?

Respect that, and do your own work anyway. You can attend family education, go to support groups and prepare the home without joint sessions. Many people feel differently about family involvement as treatment goes on.

### What should we tell relatives and friends?

Your loved one’s story is mostly theirs to tell, but you deserve support. Agree as a family on a simple, honest line, such as “She’s getting help, and we’re hopeful,” and share more only with a few people you trust.

### Is it okay that I feel relieved?

Yes. Relief doesn’t mean you don’t love them. It means you’ve been carrying fear for a long time and finally have a moment to set it down.
