It’s 3:14 a.m. and you’re awake again, checking the location app and listening for the front door. You have to be at work in five hours. You can’t remember the last time you ate a meal without your phone face-up beside the plate.

If you’re a parent of an adult child who is using, or a spouse living with a partner’s drinking or drug use, you may know this tiredness that sleep doesn’t fix. It’s burnout from loving someone with addiction, and it’s different from ordinary stress, because the crisis never really clocks out.

This guide covers the signs of burnout, why caring for yourself actually helps your loved one, where to find support, and how to share the load so it doesn’t all land on you.

## What is caregiver burnout when someone you love has an addiction?

Caregiver burnout is the physical, emotional and mental exhaustion that builds when you spend months or years managing someone else’s crisis while setting your own needs aside.

Caring for someone with a substance use problem adds pressures other caregivers may not face: broken promises, money worries, lies you can’t prove, and a constant fear of the next phone call. Many families also carry it quietly, so they get less help from friends and neighbors at exactly the time they need it most.

## What are the signs you’re burning out?

The clearest signs are exhaustion that rest doesn’t fix, a mind that won’t stop scanning for danger, and a life that has shrunk to fit around someone else’s addiction.

### Your sleep and body

Trouble falling or staying asleep, waking at every sound, headaches, stomach trouble, getting sick more often, or skipping your own doctor visits. If stress is affecting your health, see your doctor and tell them what you’re living with.

### Hypervigilance

Hypervigilance means being on high alert all the time, like a smoke detector that keeps going off. You check their eyes, their bank account and their location. Even on a good day, you can’t relax because you’re waiting for the bad one.

### Guilt, resentment and a shrinking world

You feel guilty when you rest or enjoy anything. You snap at people who don’t deserve it, then feel worse. Friends, hobbies, exercise and your faith community have slowly dropped away.

## Why does taking care of yourself help your loved one?

A rested, steady family member makes better decisions, and your loved one needs your steadiness far more than your constant watching.

Exhausted people react. They yell, give in, or hand over money just to make the asking stop. Calmer people can hold a boundary kindly, notice when a loved one seems open to help, and enjoy the sober moments instead of bracing for the next crisis.

That’s part of the thinking behind [CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training)](/blog/craft-method-for-families), a research-based approach that teaches families to communicate positively, reward sober time, let natural consequences happen, and take care of themselves. Self-care isn’t a reward you earn after your loved one gets better. It’s part of how families help.

And self-care doesn’t have to be a spa day. It can be a 20-minute walk, a real lunch break, an hour each evening with your phone in another room, or finally saying yes to coffee with a friend.

In more than 20 years of this work, I’ve seen it many times: when the most worn-out person in a family starts getting support, the temperature in the whole house often comes down. That calm gives your loved one something steady to come back to.

## Where can families find support for themselves?

Start with a peer support group, a counselor who understands addiction, or both. You don’t have to choose just one.

- **Al-Anon** is a free support group for families and friends of people with a drinking problem.
- **Nar-Anon** is a free support group for families and friends of people who use drugs.
- **SMART Recovery Family & Friends** is a science-based, non-12-step option built on CRAFT ideas.
- **A counselor or therapist,** ideally one with experience helping families affected by substance use. Ask about that directly when you call.

Try more than one meeting before deciding it isn’t for you. Groups have different personalities, and many offer online options. Our meeting finder can help you look for groups near you.

## How can the family share the load?

Divide the jobs so no single person is the family’s full-time crisis manager. In many families, one person — often a mother or a spouse — quietly becomes the hub for every call, appointment and request. That arrangement burns people out.

Jobs you can share:

- **Overnight phone duty.** Take turns. On your nights off, use your phone’s Do Not Disturb or Focus settings so only the people you choose can ring through.
- **Appointments and rides.**
- **Money decisions,** made together rather than by whoever happens to get asked.
- **Talking with professionals,** with one agreed point person.
- **Checking on everyone else,** including siblings and grandparents.

Asking can be the hardest part. Try: **“I can’t be the only one holding this anymore. Can you take Tuesday and Thursday nights so I can sleep?”** If you’re the spouse, lean on your own siblings, parents and friends too. You weren’t meant to carry this alone.

## What if you’re in danger or at your limit?

Burnout can slide into hopelessness, and some homes aren’t physically safe. Please take both seriously.

- If your partner or adult child threatens, hurts or controls you, your safety comes first. Don’t confront them or issue an ultimatum without a safety plan. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233.
- If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. That includes you. If you’ve started to feel hopeless or have thoughts of not wanting to live, 988 is there for you, too.
- SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7), can point families toward treatment and support services.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Caregiver Burnout and Addiction

### Is it selfish to focus on myself while my loved one is struggling?

No. Taking care of yourself keeps you able to help, and it shows your loved one what healthy living looks like. You can love someone deeply and still go to bed on time.

### How can I rest when I’m afraid something will happen while I’m not watching?

Constant watching can’t prevent everything, even though it feels like it should. Make a calm plan for emergencies, share overnight duty, and then give yourself permission to sleep.

### What if my spouse won’t help share the load?

Start with what you can control: your own support, sleep and boundaries. [Disagreeing about how to help](/blog/parents-disagree-about-addicted-son) is one of the most common struggles I see between parents and partners, and an outside counselor can help you find middle ground.

### How long does it take to recover from burnout?

It varies, and it usually happens in small steps rather than all at once. One full night of sleep, one meeting, one dinner with a friend. Those add up.
