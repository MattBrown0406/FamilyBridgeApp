It's 11:40 on a Tuesday night when your phone lights up. Your ex has texted: "Did you give Tyler money again?" You didn't. But you did let him sleep on your couch last weekend, and you didn't mention it. Ten minutes later you're arguing about something from 2015, and your son isn't part of the conversation at all.

Co-parenting a child with addiction is hard enough when parents share one roof. After a divorce or separation, it can feel impossible. You may have two households, two sets of rules, new partners, old resentments, and a son or daughter who knows exactly which door to knock on for which kind of help.

This guide is for divorced and separated parents, stepparents, and the grandparents caught in the middle. We'll cover how to share facts without reopening the marriage, build one plan across two homes, and respond when your ex won't cooperate. You don't have to like each other again. You just have to be on the same side of this one thing.

## Why is co-parenting a child with addiction so hard after divorce?

Addiction thrives in the gaps between people, and divorce creates gaps by design. Two households mean two sets of information, two sets of rules, and [two places to go when one parent says no](/blog/addict-playing-family-members-against-each-other).

### Two homes, two versions of the truth

Each parent sees only their side. Mom notices the missed shifts at work. Dad notices the empty bottles after a weekend visit. Neither has the full picture, and each assumes the other is overreacting or not paying attention.

### [Old wounds get in the way](/blog/parents-disagree-about-addicted-son)

Every conversation about your child can carry the weight of the marriage. "You were always too soft." "You were never there." In more than 20 years of working with families, I've watched divorced parents spend an entire session arguing about a custody schedule from a decade ago while their son sat in the waiting room, high. The hurt is real. It just can't be in charge.

### Your child learns the map

This isn't about your child being a bad person. Addiction pushes people toward whatever path keeps the using going, and in a split family that path is easy to find:

- "Dad already said it was fine."
- Moving to the "easier" house when rules tighten at the other one
- Asking a stepparent or grandparent to quietly cover a bill
- Telling each parent that the other one is the reason things are hard

## How do divorced parents agree on the facts?

Start with facts, not opinions or blame. Agree to share what you've each observed, such as dates, money, and behavior, in writing, and leave the history of the marriage out of it.

### Build one shared fact list

Keep one page you can both add to. Write down what happened, when, and who saw it: missed work, money requested and given, health scares, things found. Use one simple rule: no adjectives. "He asked me for $300 on October 12 for car repairs" is a fact. "He's lying to me again" is an argument waiting to happen.

### Be honest about what you each gave

Shame keeps many divorced parents quiet about what they've handed over: a couch, a phone bill, gas money, a "loan." Put it all on the list. The goal isn't to catch the other parent. It's to stop working against each other without meaning to.

## How can we make one plan across two households?

You don't need identical homes, but you need the same core plan. Agree on a short list of non-negotiables that hold true at both houses, and decide in advance how each of you will respond to the most likely requests.

### Agree on three to five shared rules

For example:

- No cash goes directly to our child; we pay specific bills only, and we tell each other when we do
- No using or being high in either home
- Neither of us agrees to anything new without saying, "Let me check with your mom (or dad) first"
- We both support treatment, and we say so in the same words

### Script the "other parent" moment

The most important sentence in co-parenting a child with addiction may be this one: "Your dad and I have talked, and we're on the same page. I'm not going to decide this alone." Practice it until it comes out calmly. When your child hears the same answer from both homes, the back doors start to close.

## Who does what when there are stepparents, new partners, and grandparents?

Everyone who plays a real part in your child's life needs to know the plan, but not everyone needs the same job. Clear roles keep anyone from becoming a secret back door.

### Give each person a role

- **Point parent for professionals:** one parent is the main contact for the counselor, treatment team, or interventionist and shares updates with the other within 24 hours
- **Money keeper:** tracks any financial help from any household
- **Steady presence:** often a stepparent or grandparent who keeps warm contact without negotiating about rules or money
- **Sibling checker:** someone who makes sure brothers and sisters aren't forgotten in the crisis

### Stepparents: support, don't lead

Stepparents often see clearly because they have some distance, but hard messages usually land better from a biological parent. A stepparent's job is to back the plan, not enforce it alone.

### Grandparents and the quiet cash

Grandparents often step in with money out of love, especially after a divorce. Invite them into the plan instead of scolding them, and ask, "Can we count on you to send requests back to us?"

## What if my ex won't cooperate?

You can only control your own household and your own responses. Keep offering the facts and the plan in writing, hold your side steadily, and bring in professional help to bridge the gap when you can't do it yourselves.

- Put requests in writing: short, calm, and factual
- Never use your child as the messenger between homes
- Hold your own boundaries even if your ex's are different
- Ask a neutral third party, such as a family therapist, interventionist, or mediator, to help you both meet in the middle
- Avoid criticizing the other parent to your child, even when it's deserved

A note on safety: if your relationship with your ex involved threats, intimidation, or control, your safety comes first. You don't need to meet in person or share more than necessary. Communicate through a lawyer, a mediator, or a written co-parenting tool, and consider talking with an advocate at the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233).

## How can our family put this into practice together?

Set up a simple routine that runs the same way every week, no matter whose weekend it is. Consistency is what turns two households into one team.

1. **Hold a 20-minute [weekly check-in](/blog/how-to-run-a-family-meeting-about-addiction).** A phone or video call at the same time each week is enough. Stick to an agenda.
2. **Update the shared fact list first.** Both parents add what they saw before the call, so you start from facts instead of memory.
3. **Review every request.** Go over anything your child asked either household for, and how it was answered.
4. **Confirm roles and next steps.** Who's calling the counselor? Who's driving to Thursday's appointment?
5. **Name one thing that's going right.** It might be about your child, or about how the two of you handled something this week.
6. **Follow up in writing within 24 hours.** A two-line summary keeps anyone from remembering the call differently.

## How do I take care of myself through this?

Get support that is just for you, separate from your ex and separate from your child. Co-parenting a child with addiction can stir up grief about your child and about the marriage at the same time, and you need a place to set both down.

- [Find your own support meeting](/meetings), such as Al-Anon, Nar-Anon, or SMART Recovery Family & Friends
- Talk with a counselor of your own, not just the family therapist
- Keep contact with your ex focused and time-limited
- Let go of being right about the past; you are working on the next six months

As someone in long-term recovery, I can tell you that what mattered most to me wasn't my family agreeing about everything. It was knowing they couldn't be split apart.

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Co-Parenting a Child With Addiction

### Do we have to agree on everything to help our child?

No. You need to agree on a few core things: the facts, the main rules, and the treatment goal. Your parenting styles and your feelings about each other can stay different. What matters is that your child hears the same answer from both homes on the things that count.

### Should we tell our child that we're talking to each other?

Yes. Saying "Your mom and I talk every week" removes a lot of the guesswork and the back doors. It isn't a threat. It's a calm statement that the family is working together.

### What if my ex is also drinking or using?

Focus on what you can control: the plan in your own home and your own support. Share facts about your child, but don't make your ex's use the topic of every check-in. If a minor's safety is at risk during visits, talk with a family law attorney.

### Can a family therapist or interventionist work with divorced parents?

Yes, and many have deep experience doing it. A neutral professional keeps the conversation on your child instead of the past, and can meet with each parent separately if sharing a room isn't workable.

## You don't have to be married to be on the same team

Your marriage may be over, but your family isn't. Your child still has parents, and maybe stepparents and grandparents, who want them well. When you stop being separate voices and act as one steady team, your child gets something addiction can't easily get around.
