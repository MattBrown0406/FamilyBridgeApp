It’s late. Your son just walked out the door again, and the argument starts before his car leaves the driveway. “You’re too easy on him.” “You’re too hard on him.” An hour later, the two of you aren’t talking about him anymore. You’re talking about each other.

If you’ve ever typed “parents disagree about addicted son” into a search bar at one in the morning, you are not alone, and you are not failing. In more than 20 years of working with families, I’ve rarely met two parents who saw a child’s drug or alcohol use exactly the same way. Disagreement usually doesn’t mean one of you doesn’t care. It means you both care, in different ways.

This guide covers why parents end up on opposite sides, what the split costs, and how to get on the same page without one of you having to “win.”

## Why do parents disagree so much about a son who is using?

Parents usually disagree because each one is trying to protect their son from a different danger. One is afraid of losing him to the substance. The other is afraid of losing him by pushing too hard, or of watching him never grow up.

Those fears come from real places:

- **Different histories.** A parent who grew up around drinking, or who is in recovery, may spot warning signs sooner and react more strongly.
- **Different information.** The parent your son texts late at night often knows things the other doesn’t.
- **Different temperaments.** Some people run toward a problem. Others need time before they can face it.

There’s also a pattern I see constantly: the softer one parent gets, the firmer the other becomes to balance things out, and then the first softens even more. Over months, you drift further apart than either of you would be alone.

## What does it cost when parents stay divided?

A divided home gives the addiction room to grow, because your son learns that one parent’s “no” isn’t the final answer. It also wears down your marriage and the rest of the family.

- **Quiet rescues.** Consequences get softened in secret, so the cost of using never quite reaches him.
- **Easy openings.** When parents are split, it’s easy for a loved one to [play family members against each other](/blog/addict-playing-family-members-against-each-other), often without fully planning to.
- **A marriage under strain.** Many parents tell me the fighting between them hurts more than the using itself.

## Does one of us have to be right?

No. You don’t need to agree on everything. You need to agree on the next few steps and deliver them with one voice.

Each of your instincts holds something true. The protective parent is right that connection matters; a son who feels welcome when he’s sober has a reason to move toward the family. The firmer parent is right that rescuing him from every consequence keeps everything the same. Family approaches like CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training) use exactly that mix: stay warm when your son is sober, and let the natural results of using land on him, not you. The answer isn’t soft or hard. It’s kind and clear, and it takes both of you.

## How do we get on the same page without one of us “winning”?

Talk with each other first, away from your son, and aim for a few small decisions you can both live with instead of a grand plan.

### Pick a calm time, not a crisis

Not at midnight after a slammed door. Choose 30 minutes when neither of you has been drinking and phones are down.

### Say your biggest fear, then let the other person repeat it back

Each of you finishes the sentence “What scares me most is…” The other repeats it back before responding. This often lowers the temperature more than anything, because each parent feels heard:

“So you’re afraid that if we shut off his phone, we won’t know whether he’s alive. Did I get that right?”

### Write down what you already agree on

It’s usually more than you think: you both want him alive, getting help, and not driving after using. Start there.

### Decide only the next one or two issues

Skip “everything about our son.” Pick something concrete, like what you’ll do the next time he asks for money. Try the decision for two to four weeks, then review it together.

### Agree on a tie-breaker

Decide in advance how you’ll settle the times you still disagree. Some couples default to the more cautious choice on safety. Some take a 48-hour pause. Others ask a counselor or interventionist. A plan you can both follow beats a perfect plan only one of you believes in.

## What if my partner keeps going around the plan?

Assume fear before betrayal, and bring it back to the agreement instead of fighting about it in front of your son.

- **Say what happened without blame.** “On Tuesday you gave him forty dollars after we agreed on no cash. I’m not trying to start a fight. I want to understand what happened.”
- **Ask what made it hard, and adjust if needed.** Often it was a frightening text at the worst moment. If one of you can’t follow the plan, the plan needs changing, not the parent.
- **Don’t secretly “balance it out.”** Taking away the car to make up for the forty dollars just restarts the cycle.

One exception: if disagreements in your home involve threats, intimidation or one person controlling another, whether it’s your partner or your son, that’s a safety issue, not a parenting difference. Your safety comes first. Don’t confront anyone or deliver an ultimatum without a safety plan. The National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233, can help you make one.

## How can we put this into practice as a family?

Write down what you agreed on, share it with every adult involved, and review it every week.

Use plain if-then language about your own actions: “If you ask either of us for money, we’ll answer you together within a day.” Include grandparents or older siblings your son turns to, and use [a calm weekly family meeting](/blog/how-to-run-a-family-meeting-about-addiction) to review what’s working.

## How do I take care of myself while we work this out?

Protect your own health, sleep and support, because an exhausted parent can’t hold any plan for long.

- **Get support of your own.** Al-Anon, Nar-Anon and other family groups are full of parents who have been where you are. You can go together or separately.
- **Protect couple time that isn’t about your son.** Even a 20-minute walk with a “no son talk” rule counts.
- **See a counselor if the fighting feels constant.** Helping your relationship isn’t giving up on your son.

Some things are worth agreeing on no matter who is “right.” If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, is free, confidential and available 24/7. Naloxone (Narcan), which can reverse an opioid overdose, is available without a prescription at most U.S. pharmacies.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Parents Who Disagree About an Addicted Son

### Is it normal for parents to disagree about their son’s drug or alcohol use?

Yes. It’s one of the most common struggles families bring to me. Different fears, histories and personalities almost guarantee some disagreement. What matters is turning it into a shared plan.

### Should we hide our disagreements from our son?

You don’t need to pretend you’re identical, and he probably knows you differ. What helps is presenting decisions together: “Your dad and I see some things differently, but we’ve agreed on this.”

### What if we’re divorced or separated?

You can still agree in writing on a few key things, such as money, safety and support for treatment. Keep your son out of the middle, and consider a mediator if talking keeps turning into arguing.

### When should we get professional help?

Reach out if you’ve been stuck for months, if your son’s use is getting worse, or if anyone’s safety is at risk. An interventionist or family therapist can help you build one plan you both believe in.

## You Don’t Have to Agree on Everything to Stand Together

Two loving parents who disagree are not the problem. A family that stops talking to itself is. When your different instincts become one calm, shared plan, your son hears a message he can trust: we love you, and we’re in this together.
