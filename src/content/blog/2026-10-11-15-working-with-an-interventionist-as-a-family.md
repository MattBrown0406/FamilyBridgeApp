You finally made the call. Maybe it was to an interventionist, maybe to a treatment center you found at midnight. Now there are intake coordinators, therapists, case managers and a stack of forms, and you can’t tell whether you’re supposed to be in the room or out of the way.

Working with an interventionist as a family, and with the treatment team that follows, can bring huge relief. It can also leave you feeling sidelined, especially when privacy rules mean nobody will tell you anything.

This guide explains what each professional does, how families and professionals can work as one team, what consent means in plain words, how to stay involved as your loved one moves from one level of care to the next, and which questions to ask.

## What does an interventionist actually do?

An interventionist helps a family get organized, get on the same page, and [invite their loved one into treatment](/blog/when-loved-one-is-ready-for-treatment) in a loving, well-prepared way.

That usually includes learning your family’s history, teaching everyone what helps and what doesn’t, helping each person prepare what they’ll say (often in a letter), recommending the right level of care, and coordinating admission. Some interventionists stay involved afterward as case managers or family recovery coaches.

In my own work, the conversation with the loved one is a small part of the job. Most of it happens before that day: helping a divided family become a united one. Be cautious of anyone who suggests forcing your loved one into a vehicle or using shame and threats to get them there. That isn’t help.

## What do treatment centers, therapists and family programs do?

Treatment centers provide structured care, therapists help with the emotional and mental health side, and family programs teach and support loved ones. Most people see several of these professionals along the way.

- **Detox (withdrawal management):** medical care to get through withdrawal safely. Alcohol and sedative withdrawal in particular can be dangerous without medical help.
- **Treatment centers:** residential programs, where your loved one lives on site, and outpatient programs, where they live at home and attend sessions.
- **Therapists and counselors:** individual, couples or family therapy, often including care for anxiety, depression or trauma.
- **Family programs:** many treatment centers offer education, workshops or family sessions for loved ones. If one is offered, go.
- **Sober living homes and recovery coaches:** structure and support after formal treatment.

If you don’t know where to start, SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357 (free, confidential, 24/7), can help you find treatment options.

## How can families and professionals work as one team?

Choose one family point person, share what you know honestly, agree on how updates will work, and stick to the plan you agreed on, even when it’s hard.

- **One point person.** Six relatives calling the therapist creates confusion. Pick one, and have them share updates with everyone else.
- **A one-page history.** Write down substances used and for how long, past treatment, mental health diagnoses, current medications, legal issues and what has helped before. You know things no intake form will catch.
- **Consistent messages.** If the team recommends sober living after residential care, don’t quietly offer the guest room. Mixed messages give addiction a gap to slip through.
- **Disagreements go to the professional,** not to your loved one.
- **Do your own work,** too: attend family sessions and get support for yourself.

## What does consent mean when it comes to sharing information?

For an adult, the treatment team generally needs your loved one’s written permission before sharing information with you. That permission usually comes on a form called a consent or release of information.

Here’s what that means in plain words. This is general information, not legal advice.

- HIPAA is the federal law that protects health information. Many addiction treatment programs also follow a separate federal rule with extra protections for substance use treatment records, often called “Part 2.” Without consent, a program may not even be able to confirm your loved one is there.
- A consent form can usually be specific about who can receive information, what kind, and for how long. Your loved one can usually change their mind later.
- Privacy rules limit what professionals can tell you, not what you can tell them. You can always share what you know.
- If your loved one is a minor, different rules apply, and they vary by state.

Ask each program to walk you through its forms, and talk to a lawyer if you have legal questions. When you ask your loved one, respect their dignity: **“I’m not asking to know what you say in therapy. I’d like to be able to talk with your team about how you’re doing and how we can help at home. Would you be willing to sign a release for that?”**

## How do families stay involved through care transitions?

Start asking about the next step early, well before discharge, because the hand-offs between levels of care are where it’s easiest for people to fall through the cracks.

- Ask about the aftercare plan in the first week or two, not on the last day.
- Make sure the next provider has a first appointment on the calendar, with a date, time and name, before discharge.
- Check whether consent forms need updating, since a release often covers only one provider.
- Agree ahead of time on what you’ll say if your loved one calls wanting to leave early. For example: **“We love you, and we support the next step your team recommended. We’ll help you get there.”**

Leaving treatment early can carry real risk. After time without using, tolerance drops, so a return to old amounts can cause an overdose. If opioids are involved, keep naloxone (Narcan) on hand; it is available without a prescription at most U.S. pharmacies. If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

## What questions should we ask a professional?

Ask about qualifications, how they’ll communicate with the family, how they’re paid, and what happens after the first step.

### For an interventionist

- What training and certification do you have, and how can I verify it?
- How will you prepare our family, and who should be involved?
- Do you receive any payment from programs you might recommend?
- What happens if our loved one says no?
- Do you stay involved after admission?

### For a treatment program or therapist

- Is the program licensed, and what are the staff’s credentials?
- With consent, how often will the family get updates, and from whom?
- What does family involvement look like here?
- Do you offer medications for addiction treatment and mental health care when appropriate?
- When does discharge planning start?

## Frequently Asked Questions About Working With an Interventionist as a Family

### Do we need an interventionist, or can we do this ourselves?

Some families can open the conversation on their own. An interventionist is especially worth considering when the family is divided, past attempts have gone badly, there are safety concerns, or mental health issues are part of the picture.

### What if my loved one won’t sign a consent form?

You can still share information with the team, take part in family support, and ask again later without pressure. A refusal today isn’t a refusal forever, especially once trust starts to rebuild.

### How involved should we be while our loved one is in treatment?

[Be involved in the family side of recovery](/blog/what-families-should-do-while-loved-one-in-rehab): family sessions, your own support and getting ready for what comes next. Let the team handle the clinical care, and resist managing every detail.

### Is it okay to disagree with a professional’s recommendation?

Yes. Ask questions, ask for their reasoning, and get a second opinion if you need one. Just bring the disagreement to the professional, not to your loved one, so the family stays united.
