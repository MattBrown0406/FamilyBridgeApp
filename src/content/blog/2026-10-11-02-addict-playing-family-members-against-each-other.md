Your daughter calls and tells you, her dad, that Mom already agreed to cover her car insurance this month. You call your wife. She says she told your daughter she’d “think about it.” By the time you hang up, your daughter has called her grandmother, said you’re both “being cruel,” and asked Grandma for the money instead.

If you’ve searched for “addict playing family members against each other,” you know this feeling: confused, a little foolish, and somehow angry at everyone except the substance. In this guide I’ll say “your loved one,” because the person doing this is still your daughter, son or spouse. The behavior usually comes from the illness, not from who they really are.

Here’s what this pattern is, why it happens, and how your family can answer with one calm voice.

## What does it mean when a loved one plays family members against each other?

It means your loved one deals with the family through separate one-on-one conversations, so no one has the full picture and each person can be persuaded alone. Counselors call this *triangulation*: two people communicating through a third person instead of directly. A close cousin is *splitting*, which casts one relative as “the good one” and another as “the bad one.”

It often looks like this:

- Going to the softest family member first, often a grandparent or the parent who struggles most to say no
- “Mom said yes” or “Dad already agreed”
- Secrets: “Please don’t tell Dad about this.”
- Carrying messages: “Grandma thinks you’re being too harsh.”
- Breaking one big request into small ones: gas money from a brother, a phone bill from an aunt, “just groceries” from you

## Why does a loved one with a substance use problem do this?

Mostly because it works. When someone depends on a substance, getting what they need feels urgent, and people under that pressure get very good at finding the path of least resistance.

That doesn’t make your loved one a bad person. People in recovery often say that from the inside, it didn’t feel like manipulation. It felt like survival. Families make it easier without meaning to: relatives stop comparing notes because they’re tired, embarrassed or trying not to “cause drama.”

After more than two decades of sitting with families, I can tell you this pattern rarely stops because the person using decides to stop it. It stops when the family can no longer be split.

## Why does it hurt the whole family so much?

Because it turns the people who love your loved one into opponents, and a family fighting itself can’t hold any plan.

- **Trust between relatives wears thin.** Everyone starts wondering what the others are hiding.
- **Roles harden.** The “soft” relative gets worn down, sometimes financially drained. The “strict” one becomes the villain and may pull away.
- **No one sees the whole picture.** When one person knows about missed shifts and another knows about new friends, warning signs slip through. That’s not just a family problem. It can be a safety problem.

## How do we answer with one voice?

Agree as a family that no one answers a significant request alone, that you check with each other before responding, and that [money and safety are never kept secret](/blog/family-secrets-and-addiction).

### Make a “check with each other” rule

Anything over a small dollar amount, and any change to a house rule, gets talked over before anyone says yes. Decide together what “small” means.

### Use the same short answer every time

The exact words matter less than everyone using them: “I don’t decide things like that by myself. I’ll talk with Mom, and we’ll get back to you tomorrow.”

### Verify kindly when you hear “Mom said yes”

Don’t argue about what Mom did or didn’t say. Just check: “Okay. I’ll talk to her and call you back.” Over time, the claim stops being useful.

### Step out of the messenger role

When you hear “Tell your sister…,” gently hand it back: “That sounds important. I think you should tell her yourself.”

### Decide what “no secrets” means

Not every private feeling has to be shared, but money, safety and treatment stay in the open. You might say: “I love you, and I won’t keep secrets about money or your safety from the family. I’m always glad to listen, but I may need to share it.”

## Who should be part of the plan?

Every adult your loved one turns to for money, rides, a place to stay or sympathy, not just parents.

That can include grandparents, adult siblings, aunts and uncles, or an ex-spouse. Families often leave out the softest member to spare them stress, which makes that person the easiest target. Invite them in, without shaming anyone for past yeses, and give them a simple role.

One voice also means one tone. If the firm relative delivers the message with contempt and the gentle one delivers it in tears, your loved one still hears two messages. Aim for calm and warm from everyone.

## What should we say when our loved one gets angry about this?

Stay calm, keep it short and repeat the same message. You don’t have to win the argument; you only have to stay consistent.

- “I hear that you’re frustrated. We still make these decisions together.”
- “I’m not going to argue about it. I love you, and the answer is the same.”

Skip the lectures and the list of past lies. Those turn a calm limit into a fight.

Notice the opposite, too. When your loved one asks the family directly or tells a hard truth, say so: “Thank you for coming to both of us.” Approaches like CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training) teach families to reward honest, sober behavior, because people tend to repeat what gets noticed.

If anger ever turns into threats, damage or control, your safety comes first. Don’t confront your loved one or deliver an ultimatum without a safety plan; the National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233, can help you make one. If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

## How do I take care of myself if I’m the one being targeted?

Stop carrying it alone. Tell the rest of the family what you’re being asked for, and get support for yourself.

Being the “soft one” isn’t a weakness. Often it’s love without backup. It’s normal to feel guilty the first few times you check with others before answering. You aren’t betraying your loved one; you’re protecting both of you. Al-Anon and Nar-Anon meetings are good places to talk with people who understand, and SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, offers free, confidential treatment information 24/7.

## Frequently Asked Questions About a Loved One Playing Family Members Against Each Other

### Is my loved one doing this on purpose?

Sometimes partly, often not fully. Addiction pushes people toward whatever meets their needs fastest. You don’t have to figure out intent to respond well. You only need to respond together.

### What if a relative refuses to get on board?

Keep inviting them, without pressure or blame, and focus on what the rest of you can hold. Treating that relative as the enemy just creates a new triangle.

### Should we confront our loved one about this as a family?

Not with a surprise confrontation. A calm, simple statement works better: “We’ve realized we haven’t been talking to each other enough. From now on, we’ll make these decisions together.”

### Does checking with each other mean we don’t trust our loved one?

It means you trust your family’s process. It also protects your loved one, who has fewer chances to get stuck in a story that later falls apart.

### Can this pattern continue in recovery?

Old habits can linger even when someone is doing well. Keep talking directly with each other, and expect it to ease [as trust is rebuilt step by step](/blog/rebuilding-trust-after-addiction-as-a-family).
