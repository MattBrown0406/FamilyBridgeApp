Your son calls at 2 a.m. after being pulled over. Or your sister misses her daughter’s birthday party and calls you the next day in tears, saying, “I don’t know what I’m doing anymore.” Something in the voice is different this time.

Moments like these leave families wondering how to know when a loved one is ready for treatment, and what to do in the few hours or days when they might be. Many families have already lived through a moment like this and watched it pass, because nobody had a plan ready.

This guide covers what readiness really looks like, when windows of opportunity tend to open, the signs of openness, what to have ready ahead of time, who should ask and how, and what to do if the answer is no.

## What does “ready for treatment” actually look like?

Readiness is rarely a sudden, total change of heart. More often it’s a short window when the pain of using feels bigger than the comfort of using, and that window can open and close within a day.

Families often wait for their loved one to announce, “I need help.” That does happen, but readiness usually looks messier. Your loved one may say “maybe” in the morning and “I’m fine” by dinner. That back-and-forth is normal: part of them wants to stop and part of them doesn’t. You don’t have to wait until the doubt is gone. You need to be ready when the part that wants help is louder.

Of all the families I’ve worked with over the past 20-plus years, the regret I hear most isn’t that they asked too soon. It’s that their loved one reached out once, and nobody had anything lined up.

## When do windows of opportunity tend to open?

Most often right after a painful consequence, during a stretch of remorse, or when your loved one asks for help, even indirectly.

- **After a consequence:** an arrest, a job loss, a breakup, an ER visit, a frightening health warning or a partner moving out.
- **During remorse:** the apologetic morning, the “I’m so sorry” text, or the day after missing something that mattered, like a child’s game.
- **When they ask, directly or sideways:** “I think I need to slow down.” “I can’t sleep without it anymore.” Even a question about a friend who went to treatment can be a quiet way of asking about themselves.

## What are the signs a loved one may be open to help?

Watch for changes in how they talk about their use, not just for promises. Openness tends to show up in small shifts:

- They talk about their use as their problem, not just your problem with it.
- They’re less defensive when the subject comes up.
- They admit they’ve tried to cut back and couldn’t.
- They ask about treatment: how long it takes, what it’s like, what happens to their job.
- They seem worried about their health, their kids or a relationship.

None of these guarantees a yes. They mean it’s worth asking. One good week, or a promise made in the middle of an argument, is not the same thing.

## What should we have ready before a window opens?

Everything you’d need to act the same day: a few programs already called, insurance checked, and a plan for who drives. If your answer to “Okay, I’ll go” is “Let me look into it,” the moment can slip away.

- **Programs called.** Call two or three ahead of time. Ask whether they have openings, what level of care they offer, whether they take your insurance, and how admission works.
- **Insurance checked.** Call the member services number on the insurance card and ask about substance use treatment benefits, including whether prior authorization is needed.
- **Medical safety.** If your loved one drinks heavily every day or uses benzodiazepines or other sedatives, stopping suddenly can be dangerous. Ask the program or a doctor whether medically supervised detox is needed.
- **Logistics.** Decide who drives, who covers pets, kids or work calls, and what goes in the bag: ID, insurance card, medications in their original bottles and a few days of clothes.
- **A backup.** Know your second choice if the first program has no opening.

If you need help finding programs, SAMHSA’s National Helpline is free, confidential and available 24/7 at 1-800-662-4357.

## Who should ask, and how do we invite them?

The person your loved one trusts most and argues with least should ask. That might be a sibling, a grandparent or a friend rather than the parent who has been doing all the worrying.

[Decide this as a family ahead of time](/blog/how-to-run-a-family-meeting-about-addiction), and agree that everyone will back up the invitation rather than undercut it. Then keep it short, warm and specific:

- “I know last night scared you. I’ve already talked to a program that has a spot this week, and Dad can drive you tomorrow morning. Would you be willing to go?”
- “Would you be willing to call them with me right now, just to ask a few questions?”

Don’t ask while they’re intoxicated, don’t surprise them with a crowd, and don’t back the invitation with threats. If you think your family needs a more structured conversation, work with a [certified interventionist](/blog/working-with-an-interventionist-as-a-family) who will plan it carefully and with love.

## What if they say no?

Treat a no as “not yet,” not as failure. Stay warm, keep the offer open, and [keep making sober time rewarding](/blog/craft-method-for-families) while letting the consequences of use land.

You might say: “Okay. The offer stands, and I love you either way.” Then leave it alone. Don’t punish the no with silence or anger; the next window opens more easily when this one closed gently. Check in with the programs every few weeks so your plan stays current.

## How do you take care of yourself while you wait?

Keep living your own life while you stay ready. Waiting can swallow every thought you have, and an exhausted family is more likely to miss a window or push too hard when it comes.

Lean on a support group or counselor, and keep naloxone (Narcan) at home if opioids are involved; it’s available without a prescription at most U.S. pharmacies. If your loved one has threatened, hurt or controlled anyone, your safety comes first, and no invitation or ultimatum should happen without a safety plan. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Knowing When a Loved One Is Ready for Treatment

### Can treatment help if they weren’t fully ready when they went?

Yes, it can. Many people start treatment because of pressure from family, work or a court, then begin to engage once they’re sober and around people who understand. Motivation often grows during treatment, not only before it.

### Should we wait for them to hit rock bottom?

No. Waiting for “rock bottom” is risky, because for some people the bottom is an overdose or a tragedy. Families can help create openings much earlier through steady, loving responses and natural consequences.

### What if they say yes and then change their mind?

That’s common. Keep the time between yes and admission as short as possible, ideally the same day or the next. If they back out, stay calm and leave the door open.

### Who should drive them to treatment?

Someone calm, sober and unlikely to start an argument on the way. It doesn’t have to be the person who asked. Some programs can help arrange transportation, so ask when you call.
