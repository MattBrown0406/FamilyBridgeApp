You send a text on Sunday night: “Thinking of you. Love you.” It shows as delivered, then nothing. By Wednesday you call and it goes straight to voicemail. Then your daughter mentions, almost by accident, that her brother texted her a meme yesterday. He’s talking. Just not to you.

When an addicted loved one cuts off contact, the silence can feel worse than the arguments ever did. Parents lie awake wondering if he’s alive. Spouses replay the last fight. And the one family member he still answers suddenly finds herself carrying everyone’s questions, fears and messages.

This guide is for the whole family: the people who’ve been shut out and the person who’s still let in. We’ll cover why people in active addiction pull away, what to do (and not do) with the contact you still have, how to keep one family voice instead of five, and how to look after yourselves while you wait.

## Why does an addicted loved one cut off contact with family?

Most people in active addiction pull away from family to avoid shame, pressure and questions they can’t answer. The cutoff is usually about protecting the use, not about how much they love you.

In more than 20 years of working with families, I’ve rarely seen someone go silent because they stopped caring. Far more often, every call from home feels like a test they know they’ll fail.

### Why they answer some people and not others

People usually keep the line open with whoever feels safest: a sibling who doesn’t lecture, a grandparent who doesn’t ask hard questions, a cousin who doesn’t report back. That person isn’t a traitor to the family plan. They’re an asset, as long as the family doesn’t burn them out or turn them into a spy.

## What should we do in the first few days after they go silent?

First, check on safety. Then agree as a family on what you know and what you don’t, before anyone reacts alone. A calm, shared response in the first week prevents a lot of damage later.
- **Confirm they’re okay.** Ask the family member who still has contact for a simple check: “Is he safe? Does he have a place to sleep?” That’s enough for now.
- **Share facts, not theories.** One person collects what’s actually known (last contact, who, what was said) and shares it with everyone.
- **Pause the solo outreach.** Five relatives sending five worried texts feels like a pile-on, and it usually deepens the silence.
- **Avoid dramatic moves.** Showing up unannounced or posting on social media can close doors you’ll want open later.

## How can the family member who still has contact help, without being caught in the middle?

The person who still has contact is a bridge, not a messenger or a detective. Their job is to keep the relationship alive and pass along only what the family agreed to share. Protecting that connection matters more than getting information from it.

### Let them stay the safe person

If your loved one suspects the sibling is reporting every word to Mom, that line goes quiet too. Agree as a family that the connector shares only safety concerns and big changes, not every detail of every conversation.

### Give them one simple message to carry

Instead of “Tell him Dad’s furious” or “Tell him he’s breaking my heart,” agree on one short, warm message together. For example: “Everyone loves you. Nobody is going to ambush you. When you’re ready to talk, we’re here.”

### Watch for burnout

Being the only open door is exhausting. Ask the connector how *they’re* doing, not just what they heard, and make it clear they can step back.

## What can we say when an addicted loved one cuts off contact with us?

Keep messages short, warm and free of pressure. One brief, loving message every week or two usually works better than daily pleas. The goal is to leave the door open, not to argue your way through it.

Approaches like [CRAFT](/blog/craft-method-for-families) (Community Reinforcement and Family Training, a research-backed method that teaches families to reward healthy behavior and stop rewarding unhealthy behavior) show that positive, low-pressure contact keeps relationships alive and makes treatment more likely down the road.

Messages that tend to help:
- “No need to reply. Just wanted you to know I love you.”
- “Saw your favorite team won. Thought of you.”
- “If you ever want to grab coffee, I’d like that. No big talk.”

Messages that tend to backfire:
- “I guess you don’t care about your family anymore.”
- “Your mother is sick with worry because of you.”
- Long paragraphs about what they’re doing wrong.

If your loved one does respond, keep the first conversation light and thank them for reaching out. Save the hard conversations for later, with a plan, and learn how to [talk to someone about their drinking without fighting](/blog/talk-about-drinking-without-fighting).

## When should we worry that silence means something more serious?

Silence alone isn’t an emergency, but certain changes are. If no one, including the family member who usually hears from them, has had contact in several days, or you see signs of a medical, legal or housing crisis, it’s time to act.

Reasons to step up your response:
- The connector hasn’t heard anything either, and that’s unusual
- You know they’re using opioids or other drugs with high overdose risk
- They’ve recently lost housing, a job or a relationship
- They’ve said anything suggesting they don’t want to live

In those cases, a welfare check through local police, contacting a roommate or partner, or reaching out to their treatment provider (if they have one and signed a release) may be appropriate. Decide together, as a family, who makes that call.

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

A note on safety: sometimes distance is healthy for *you*, too. If past contact involved threats, intimidation or control, you don’t have to reopen that door. Your safety comes first, and the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) can help you plan.

## How can our family put this into practice together?

The families who handle a cutoff best treat it as a team problem with one plan. Agree on the facts, agree on the message and agree on who does what. Here’s a simple weekly routine:
1. **Hold a short family check-in.** Once a week, 20 minutes, same time. Phone or video works fine.
2. **Share facts from the connector.** Only what was agreed: safety, housing, health, anything urgent. No gossip and no interrogating.
3. **Choose one message for the week.** Decide who sends it, and keep it brief and warm. Everyone else holds off on solo outreach.
4. **Name your thresholds.** Write down what would trigger a welfare check or a call to a professional, so no one has to decide alone at 2 a.m.
5. **Check on the connector.** Ask how they’re holding up and whether the role still feels manageable.
6. **Plan for the reply.** Agree now on what you’ll say if your loved one reaches out, including how you’ll respond if they ask for money or a place to stay.

## How do I take care of myself through this?

You can’t control when your loved one picks up the phone, but you can control how you spend the waiting. Taking care of yourself isn’t giving up on them. It keeps you steady enough to respond well when they do reach out.
- **Set a worry window.** Give yourself a set time each day to check messages and feel the fear, then put the phone down.
- **Find your people.** Al-Anon, Nar-Anon, SMART Recovery Family & Friends and family support groups are full of people who know this exact silence.
- **Keep your routines.** Sleep, meals, work and time with the rest of the family still matter, especially for kids and siblings who may feel forgotten.
- **Talk to someone.** A therapist who understands addiction can help you carry grief and uncertainty without carrying it alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions About When an Addicted Loved One Cuts Off Contact

### Should I keep texting if my loved one blocked my number?

Respect the block for now rather than trying other numbers or accounts, which usually feels like pressure. Let the family member who still has contact carry your one agreed message. Blocks are often temporary.

### Is it okay to ask the sibling who still hears from him what he says?

You can ask whether he’s safe and whether anything urgent is going on. Asking for every detail puts the sibling in a bind and risks losing the last open line. Agree as a family on what gets shared and trust the connector with the rest.

### Does cutting off contact mean they hate us?

Almost never. When an addicted loved one cuts off contact, it’s usually about shame and protecting the use, not a verdict on the family.

### Should we stage an intervention to break the silence?

Possibly, but not as a surprise reaction to the silence itself. A professionally guided intervention takes planning, a united family and a treatment option ready to go. A [certified interventionist](/blog/working-with-an-interventionist-as-a-family) can help you decide whether and when it makes sense.

### How long should we wait before doing something?

There’s no single timeline. If the connector confirms they’re safe, steady low-pressure contact for weeks or even months is reasonable. If no one has heard from them in several days and that’s unusual, or there are safety concerns, act sooner.

## The door stays open because you keep it open

When an addicted loved one cuts off contact, it can feel like the whole family has lost them. But silence isn’t the end of the story. In my own recovery and in the families I’ve worked with, people who come back often say the same thing: they knew someone was still there, without a lecture waiting.
