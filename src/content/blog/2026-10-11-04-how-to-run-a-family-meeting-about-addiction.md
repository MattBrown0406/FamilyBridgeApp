You finally got everyone in one room to talk about your husband’s drinking: his parents, his sister, your two oldest kids. Ten minutes in, his mother is defending him, your daughter is in tears, and the conversation has drifted to an argument from years ago. Everyone leaves more upset than they came.

Meetings like that are common. But once you know how to run a family meeting about addiction, it can become the most useful hour of your week. In my experience, the difference is rarely the people. It’s the format.

This guide walks you through a simple weekly meeting: building the agenda, keeping it calm, making sure everyone is heard and writing down what you decide. It also covers whether to include your loved one, and what to avoid.

## Why should our family hold a regular meeting about addiction?

A short meeting on a calm day gives your family a place to make decisions, so you aren’t forced to make them in a crisis.

Without one, decisions happen at 11 p.m., by text, made by whoever happens to be awake. A weekly rhythm takes the pressure off: if something isn’t solved this week, there’s a set time to come back to it. It’s also where the quieter people in your family, like a teenager or a grandparent, finally get a turn.

When I begin working with a family, one of the first things I ask is when they last sat down together without a crisis forcing it. Many can’t remember.

## What’s a simple format for a weekly family meeting?

Keep it to 30 to 45 minutes at the same time each week, with an agenda, a facilitator, one main topic, a turn for everyone, and decisions written down.

### Before the meeting: build the agenda

Anyone can add a topic during the week. The facilitator, whose job is to keep time and keep turns, not to win, picks one main topic. Other items wait.

### Start with what’s going right (5 minutes)

Each person names one good thing, however small. He made it to work every day. Thursday’s dinner was peaceful. This isn’t pretending. It trains the family to notice progress, which matters when you’re trying to encourage more of it.

### Quick check-in (5–10 minutes)

Each person says in a sentence or two how they’re doing. No one responds or fixes anything yet.

### One main topic (15–20 minutes)

For example: “How do we handle weekend rides?” Use one simple rule: everyone speaks once before anyone speaks twice.

### Write down decisions (5 minutes)

Record what you decided, who will do what, by when, and when you’ll revisit it. Unwritten decisions tend to be remembered five different ways.

### End on time, on a kind note

Close with something to look forward to, even if it’s just ordering pizza. Ending on time makes people willing to come back.

## What ground rules keep a family meeting calm?

Agree on a few rules before the first meeting: one person talks at a time, no insults, stay on today’s topic, and anyone can call a short break.

> **• Speak for yourself.** “I was scared when you didn’t come home Saturday” lands very differently from “You’re so selfish.”
> **• Stay in the present.** Talk about this week, not every hurt from the last ten years.
> **• Keep a “parking lot.”** Write side issues down for a future agenda instead of chasing them.
> **• Phones away,** and no one drinks or uses before or during the meeting.
> **• Breaks are allowed.** Anyone can say, “I need ten minutes. I’m coming back.” And then they come back.

## Should our loved one be in the family meeting?

It depends. Many families start by meeting without their loved one to get on the same page, then invite them in once everyone can deliver the same calm message.

### Meet without your loved one when

- The family isn’t aligned yet and needs to work through disagreements
- You’re deciding boundaries, money rules or [a relapse plan](/blog/family-relapse-prevention-plan)
- People need to talk about hurt that would turn into a pile-on

Meeting without them isn’t plotting against them. It makes sure they eventually hear one clear, kind message instead of five competing ones.

### Include your loved one when

- They’re sober for the meeting
- You’re discussing shared daily life, such as schedules, chores or house rules
- The family can listen at least as much as it talks

Let your loved one add topics too. Being asked “What would you like us to talk about?” can mean a lot to someone who usually feels talked about. If they show up intoxicated, reschedule kindly: “Let’s do this tomorrow, when we can all think clearly.”

## What should we avoid in a family meeting about addiction?

Avoid turning it into a trial, a surprise confrontation, or a venting session that never reaches a decision.

> **• Don’t ambush.** Never spring a “meeting” on your loved one to corner them. If you’re considering an intervention, work with a professional who does it with care and preparation.
> **• Don’t pile on.** Five people listing failures will shut anyone down.
> **• Don’t tackle everything at once.** One topic done well beats six done badly.
> **• Don’t make threats in the heat of the moment.** [Boundaries should be decided calmly, ahead of time](/blog/boundaries-as-a-family-addicted-loved-one).
> **• Don’t skip the good weeks.** Meeting when things are calm builds the habit you’ll need when they aren’t.

If your loved one has been violent or threatening, your safety comes first. Don’t include them in meetings about boundaries, and don’t announce a big decision without a safety plan. The National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233, can help you make one.

## How can we put this into practice as a family?

Choose a day and time, pick a first facilitator, and keep the agenda and notes in one place everyone can reach.

Make your first topic an easy one: “How do we want these meetings to work?” Agree on ground rules together, and let relatives who live far away join by video.

## How do I take care of myself if I’m the one holding it all together?

Share the load. Rotate who facilitates and who takes notes, and get support that’s just for you.

Your feelings belong on the agenda as much as anyone’s. Outside the meeting, Al-Anon, Nar-Anon or a counselor can give you a place to be heard without having to run the room.

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, offers free, confidential treatment referrals and information 24/7.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Family Meetings and Addiction

### How often should we meet?

Weekly works well for most families, for 30 to 45 minutes. During a crisis you might add a short extra check-in, but keep the weekly meeting as your steady anchor.

### Should young children attend?

Young children don’t need adult details, but they do need to know they’re safe and that it isn’t their fault. Give them simple, age-appropriate updates separately. Older teens can often join part of the meeting.

### What if a family member refuses to come?

Hold the meeting anyway, share the notes, and keep the invitation open. People often join once they see the meetings stay calm.

### What if it turns into a fight anyway?

Take a break, or end early and try again next week. One rough meeting isn’t failure. Families get better at this with practice.

### Can a professional help run our meetings?

Yes. [A family therapist or interventionist](/blog/working-with-an-interventionist-as-a-family) can facilitate the first few, especially if your family has a long history of conflict.

## A Calm Hour That Changes the Week

Families rarely get on the same page in one big conversation. They get there an hour at a time, week after week, by listening, deciding and following through together.
