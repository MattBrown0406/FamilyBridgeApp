It’s Saturday morning. Your husband is quiet and pale after another night of “just a few,” and the words are right there: we need to talk about your drinking. You also know exactly how it will go. He’ll get defensive, you’ll get louder, someone will bring up something from three years ago, and by noon nobody is speaking.

If you’re searching for how to talk to someone about their drinking without fighting, you don’t need a perfect speech. You need a way to be heard without starting a war. That’s possible, and it’s a skill you can learn, not a personality trait.

This guide covers when to talk, what to say, how to listen, what to avoid, what to do if it becomes a fight anyway, and how to get the whole family using the same calm approach.

## When is the best time to talk to someone about their drinking?

When they’re sober, rested and not rushed, and when you’re calm. Never try to have this conversation while they’re drinking or still feeling the effects.

Talking to someone who has been drinking almost never goes well. They’re quicker to anger, they may not remember it accurately, and you end up arguing with the alcohol instead of the person. Better moments include:

- Later in the day after a rough night, once they’re clear-headed and the regret is still fresh.
- A quiet, private time at home, not right before work or in front of the kids.
- A time when you’ve cooled down, not in the heat of your own anger.

Skip holidays, family gatherings, the car ride home from a party, and text messages. And if your loved one brings up their drinking first, that’s an opening. Don’t spend it on “I told you so.”

## What should I actually say?

Lead with care, describe one specific thing you saw, say how it affected you, and ask for one small thing. Short and specific works far better than long and general.

### Use “I” statements

“You drink too much” invites a debate about how much is too much. “I was scared riding home with you Friday night” is your experience, and nobody can argue with it. A simple pattern: “I felt \_\_\_ when \_\_\_. I’d like \_\_\_.”

### Keep it short

One incident, one feeling, one request. Not a list of everything that’s gone wrong in the last five years. Five or ten minutes is plenty for a first conversation.

### Phrases you can borrow

- “I love you, and I’m worried. Last night you fell asleep before the kids went to bed, and I felt really alone. Can we talk about it?”
- “I’m not trying to win an argument. I just want you to know what this has been like for me.”
- “Would you be willing to talk to your doctor about how much you’ve been drinking?”

## How do I listen without it turning into an argument?

Ask a real question, then stop talking and let them answer. Listening doesn’t mean agreeing. It means your loved one feels heard, so they don’t have to defend themselves.

- Ask open questions, such as “How do you feel about your drinking lately?”
- Reflect back what you hear: “It sounds like work has been crushing you.”
- Don’t rebut every point. If they say “I only had three,” you don’t need to count the bottles. Go back to what you saw and felt.
- Listen for what they want: better sleep, less tension at home, more energy for the kids. Their own reasons will move them more than yours.

After two decades of sitting in living rooms with families, I’ve noticed that the person who finally gets through is rarely the one with the best arguments. It’s the one their loved one feels safe being honest with.

## What should I avoid saying?

Avoid labels, lectures and ultimatums. Each one shifts the conversation away from the drinking and toward defending against you.

- **Labels.** Words like “alcoholic,” “drunk” or “you’re just like your father” give them something to argue with instead of something to think about.
- **Lectures.** Long speeches, and articles left on the counter, tend to get tuned out within a minute.
- **Ultimatums you won’t keep.** “If you drink again, I’m leaving,” said in anger, teaches them that your words don’t mean much. [A boundary decided calmly ahead of time](/blog/boundaries-as-a-family-addicted-loved-one) is different, and it’s worth planning.
- **Interrogations, sarcasm and old fights.** “How many did you have?” and “Here we go again” shut the door fast.

## What do I do if it turns into a fight anyway?

Stop the conversation, not the relationship. Call a calm pause, step away if you need to, and come back to it once you’re both settled.

You might say: “I don’t want to fight with you. Let’s stop for now and come back to this tomorrow.” Then actually stop. Don’t chase the last word down the hallway.

When you come back, repair first: “I got louder than I meant to yesterday, and I’m sorry for that part. I still care about what I was trying to say.” Owning your part makes it easier for them to own theirs. One rough conversation doesn’t erase your progress.

If a conversation ever moves into threats, breaking things, blocking a doorway or physical harm, leave and get safe. If someone threatens, hurts or controls you, your safety comes first, and no confrontation or ultimatum should happen without a safety plan. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233. If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.

## How can the whole family use the same calm approach?

Agree together on timing, tone and the few things everyone will and won’t say. When one person is calm and another is yelling, your loved one hears the yelling.

This doesn’t mean everyone confronts your loved one at once. It means that in each person’s own conversations, the message sounds the same. Meet as a family without your loved one and agree on a few basics:

- No talks about drinking while they’re drinking.
- No labels, no lectures, no threats we won’t keep.
- Every concern comes with an “I” statement and something we appreciate.
- Anyone can call a pause if things get heated.

## How do I take care of myself when the drinking hasn’t stopped?

Treat your own well-being as part of the plan, because a worn-out, resentful you will have a much harder time staying calm. You can’t control their drinking, but you can protect your sleep, your friendships and your peace.

- Talk to someone who is on your side: a counselor, a trusted friend or an Al-Anon meeting.
- If your loved one decides to cut back or quit, encourage them to talk to a doctor first. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous, and heavy daily drinkers shouldn’t stop suddenly without medical help.
- For free, confidential help finding treatment or family support, call SAMHSA’s National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357, available 24/7.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Talking to Someone About Their Drinking

### What if they say they don’t have a problem?

Don’t argue about the label. Keep coming back to specific things you saw and how they affected you. Denial is common, and a calm conversation often plants a seed that grows later, even when it doesn’t look like it worked.

### Can I bring it up by text?

Text is fine for asking for a time to talk, but not for the conversation itself. Tone gets lost, and screenshots last forever. Try: “Can we find some time this weekend to talk? I want to do it calmly.”

### How often should I bring it up?

Not every day. Have a real conversation, then let your actions do most of the talking: [warm time together when they’re sober](/blog/craft-method-for-families), and calm boundaries when they’re not. Bring it up again when a natural opening appears.

### What if I’m the one who always loses my temper?

That’s more common than you think, and it makes sense given what you’re carrying. Practice your opening lines ahead of time, give yourself permission to pause, and repair when you slip. Support for yourself makes calm much easier to find.

## You Can Be Heard Without a Fight

Calm, short, honest conversations add up, especially when everyone in the family speaks the same way. You don’t have to say it perfectly. You just have to keep saying it with love, at the right time.
