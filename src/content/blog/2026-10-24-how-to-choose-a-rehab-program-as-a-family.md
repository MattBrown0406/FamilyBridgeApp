It’s 11 p.m. Your daughter finally said, “Fine. I’ll go.” Now you’re at the kitchen table with your laptop, ten tabs open, and every website shows the same ocean view and the same promise. Your husband is on the phone with a “helpline” that won’t say which facility it works for. Your sister just texted a link to a place her coworker’s son went. Nobody knows which one is real.

Learning how to choose a rehab program usually happens at the worst possible moment: fast, scared and with the whole family weighing in at once. When five people research separately, you end up with five favorite programs, a dozen half-answered phone calls and a window of willingness that starts to close while you argue.

This guide walks your family through how to choose a rehab program together. We’ll cover what level of care your loved one may need, the questions worth asking every program, the red flags that should end a call, and how to divide the work so you can decide quickly and calmly. You can also read about [when a loved one is ready for treatment](/blog/when-loved-one-is-ready-for-treatment).

## What should our family look for first when choosing a rehab program?

Start with the right level of care, not the nicest website. A good program matches what your loved one actually needs medically and practically, and it can explain that match clearly. Location, amenities and price come after that.

In more than 20 years of working with families, the most common mistake I see is choosing a program because it looks peaceful, or because it had a bed open tonight. A peaceful setting is nice. A program that can safely manage withdrawal, treat co-occurring mental health issues and involve the family is what helps.

### Understanding levels of care

Treatment comes in different intensities. Here are the basic terms, in plain language:

- **Detox (withdrawal management):** Medical support while the body clears alcohol or drugs. Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be dangerous, so this step should never be skipped when it’s needed.

- **Residential (inpatient):** Living at the facility, usually for several weeks, with structured treatment all day.

- **Partial hospitalization (PHP):** Treatment most of the day, several days a week, while sleeping at home or in sober housing.

- **Intensive outpatient (IOP):** Several sessions a week, often in the evenings, while keeping up work or school.

- **Outpatient:** Regular counseling or medical visits, often as a step down after more intensive care.

A doctor, licensed counselor or interventionist can do an assessment to recommend a starting level. Ask for one before you commit. For more on [working with an interventionist](/blog/working-with-an-interventionist-as-a-family), read our family guide.

## What questions should we ask before we choose a rehab program?

The best programs answer specific questions directly and in writing. If a program gets vague or pushes you to decide on the phone, that tells you something. Keep the same list in front of every family member who makes calls.

### The questions that matter most

- Is the program licensed by the state, and is it accredited by The Joint Commission or CARF? (Accreditation means an outside group reviews its standards.)

- Who is on staff? Are there licensed therapists, a physician and nurses available?

- Do you treat co-occurring conditions like depression, anxiety or trauma?

- Do you offer or support medications for addiction treatment, such as buprenorphine, methadone or naltrexone for opioid use disorder?

- What does family involvement look like? Is there family programming, and how often will we hear from the team?

- What is the discharge plan? How do you help with the step down to outpatient care, sober living and local support?

- What will this cost, what does insurance cover, and what’s billed separately?

### Why the family question matters so much

A program that keeps the family at arm’s length often sends someone home to a household that hasn’t changed. Programs that include family sessions help everyone learn the same language and prepare for coming home. Ask how they handle consent and privacy so you know what you can expect to hear. Our guide to [what families should do during rehab](/blog/what-families-should-do-while-loved-one-in-rehab) explains how to stay involved.

## What are the red flags when choosing a rehab program?

Some treatment marketing is designed to capture frightened families, not help them. If a call center won’t name the facility, or a program offers free travel or promises a cure, slow down. Real programs can tolerate your questions.

Watch for these warning signs:

- **Anonymous helplines** that won’t tell you who they are or who owns the facility

- **Free flights, cash or “we’ll waive everything”** offers, which can be signs of patient brokering (being paid to steer patients to a facility)

- **Guaranteed results** or very high success rates with no explanation of how they’re measured

- **Pressure to decide in the next hour** before you can verify anything

- **No licensed medical staff** for detox, or no clear answer about who handles a medical emergency

- **Discouraging medications** that are proven to help, like buprenorphine or methadone

You can verify a facility’s license through your state’s health or behavioral health department. FindTreatment.gov, run by SAMHSA (the federal Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration), lists licensed programs, and the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357 is free and confidential.

## How can our family decide on a rehab program without fighting?

Agree on what matters before anyone falls in love with a program. Choose a short list of must-haves, assign one person to make final calls and give yourselves a deadline. Disagreement shrinks when everyone is comparing programs against the same criteria.

### Settle on your must-haves together

Most families can agree on three to five non-negotiables: safe detox if needed, licensed staff, treatment for co-occurring issues, family involvement and a real discharge plan. Write them down. When Uncle Rick says, “But this one has horses,” you can kindly point back to the list.

### Keep one voice with your loved one

Your loved one should hear one clear plan, not a debate. If Dad says “anywhere you want,” and Mom says “only this place,” the person who is struggling learns the plan is negotiable. Decide as a family first, then present the choice together, with any reasonable options you’re comfortable offering.

## How can our family put this into practice together?

Turn the research into a short, shared routine. This works best when it happens over one or two days, not two weeks, because willingness can change quickly.

1. **Hold a quick family meeting.** Agree on your must-have list, your budget and insurance facts, and who is involved in the decision.

2. **Divide the calls.** Give each person two or three programs and the same question list. One person handles insurance verification.

3. **Write down every answer in one shared place.** Note who you spoke to, what was promised and what still needs checking.

4. **Verify before you trust.** Check state licensing and accreditation for every finalist.

5. **Narrow to two and decide by your deadline.** Meet again, compare against your list and choose. Name one point person to coordinate admission.

6. **Plan the follow-up.** Decide who will join family sessions and who stays in touch with the treatment team.

## How do I take care of myself through this?

Choosing a program can feel like you’re holding someone’s life in your hands. You don’t have to carry that alone, and you don’t have to find the perfect place. You’re looking for a good, safe, honest one.

Eat something. Sleep when you can. Let someone else make a few of the calls. Talk with a friend, counselor or support group about how scared you are. Your loved one will need you for the long road of recovery, not just this week, so pace yourself now.

If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988. If conversations about treatment have turned threatening or you feel unsafe at home, put your own safety first and reach the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233.

## Frequently Asked Questions About How to Choose a Rehab Program

### How long should rehab last?

There’s no single right length. Many residential programs run 30 to 90 days, and longer engagement in some form of care is generally linked to better outcomes. What matters most is a clear plan for continuing care after the first program ends.

### Is a rehab program close to home better than one far away?

It depends. A local program makes family involvement and outpatient follow-up easier. A program farther away can help some people step away from familiar triggers. Choose based on quality and fit first, then distance.

### How do we know if a rehab program is legitimate?

Check that it’s licensed by your state and look for accreditation from The Joint Commission or CARF. Ask who owns it and who answers the phone. A legitimate program will give you clear, written answers about staff, services and costs.

### What if our loved one refuses the program we chose?

Stay calm and keep the plan steady. Sometimes offering two acceptable options gives them a sense of choice without reopening the whole decision. If refusal continues, a professional interventionist or a CRAFT-trained therapist can help your family plan next steps.

### Does insurance cover rehab?

Many health plans cover substance use treatment, though coverage varies by plan and level of care. Call your insurer directly, ask what’s in-network and get a reference number for the call. Don’t rely only on a facility’s word about your benefits.

## One Family, One Choice, One Plan

You don’t need to become an expert overnight to choose a rehab program well. You need the right questions, a little skepticism and a family willing to work from the same list. When you decide together, your loved one walks into treatment knowing everyone at home is on the same page, and that is a powerful place to start.
