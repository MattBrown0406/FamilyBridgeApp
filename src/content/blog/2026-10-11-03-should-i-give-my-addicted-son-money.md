The text arrives at 9:47 p.m.: “Mom I need $200 for rent by tomorrow or they’re kicking me out. I’ll pay you back Friday I promise.” Your stomach drops. You picture him with nowhere to sleep. You also remember the last three times he promised to pay you back.

If you’re asking yourself, “Should I give my addicted son money?” you’re caught between two real fears: that saying no will hurt him, and that saying yes will feed the very thing that’s hurting him. Both fears make sense. And you shouldn’t have to answer that question alone at 9:47 at night.

This guide covers the difference between helping and rescuing, how to handle the most common requests, how to decide as a family, and what to say.

## Should I give my son money while he’s using?

In most cases, don’t give cash, but that doesn’t mean you stop helping. Cash can be spent on anything, including drugs or alcohol, and you’ll almost never know where it went.

The real question isn’t “money or no money.” It’s “What kind of help, for what, paid how?” Many families find a middle path: no cash, but they still pay certain bills directly, buy groceries and help with costs connected to getting well.

In my work as an interventionist, money is where I see good plans fall apart most often. Not because parents are weak, but because money requests arrive wrapped in urgency, guilt and fear all at once.

## What’s the difference between helping and rescuing?

Helping supports your son’s health and growth. Rescuing removes the consequences of using, so nothing has to change. Rescuing is what people usually mean by “enabling”: doing things, out of love, that make it easier to keep using.

### Helping often looks like

- Paying for a doctor’s visit, an assessment or treatment
- Buying groceries or a bus pass to get to work

### Rescuing often looks like

- Covering debts or losses that came from using
- Replacing a phone or laptop he sold or “lost”
- Handing over cash “to tide him over,” again and again

When you’re not sure, ask: Would I pay for this if he weren’t using? Does it help him get healthier, or help him avoid feeling the cost? Am I deciding out of love or out of panic? To look honestly at your own patterns, try the free “Am I Enabling?” exercise.

## How should I handle requests for rent, a phone or a car?

If your family decides to help, pay the business directly, never your son, and only for things you agreed on ahead of time.

### Rent

Pay the landlord and ask to see the lease or the late notice. Decide in advance how long you’ll do this and what you’d like to see in return, such as an appointment with a counselor. A possible eviction can also be a moment when he’s more open to talking about help.

### Phone

Many parents keep the phone on because it’s their lifeline to knowing he’s okay. That’s a reasonable choice. Pay the carrier directly, and decide now what happens if the phone is sold.

### Car

A car is about safety as well as money. If there’s any chance he drives after using, paying for gas, insurance or repairs can put him and others at risk. If the car is in your name or you co-signed, ask your insurance agent or an attorney about your own responsibility.

### “I’ll pay you back”

Treat any money you give as a gift you can afford to lose. If it comes back, wonderful. Loans you have to collect tend to create resentment, not repayment.

### Food

Buy the food instead of sending money for it. Gift cards can be sold for cash, so groceries delivered to his door are a safer way to make sure he eats.

## Why should money decisions be made as a family?

Because a son who needs money will usually go to whoever is most likely to say yes, and one parent alone at night is the easiest yes there is.

Sit down with everyone who gets asked, including grandparents and siblings, and agree on a few simple rules:

- We don’t give cash.
- We pay bills directly to the company and keep the receipt.
- Any request over an amount we choose is decided by at least two of us.
- We answer within a day, not on the spot.
- We’ll always talk seriously about helping with the cost of treatment.

Writing it down protects the soft-hearted person in your family, who can point to the agreement instead of having to say no alone.

## What do I say when he asks for money?

Keep it short, warm and the same every time: acknowledge what he’s facing, say what you will and won’t do, and leave the door open to real help.

- “I love you, and I’m not going to send cash. If the rent is real, send me the landlord’s information and we’ll talk as a family about paying it directly.”
- “I can’t pay for that. I can bring groceries over on Saturday.”
- “If you’re ready to [talk to someone about treatment](/blog/when-loved-one-is-ready-for-treatment), I’ll help you make that call today.”

Skip the lectures and the list of past debts, and avoid “You’ll just spend it on drugs.” Even if it’s true, it starts a fight and gives him something to argue with instead of something to think about.

Expect pushback. Anger, tears and “You don’t care about me” are common, especially the first few times. You don’t have to defend yourself. Repeat your answer, end the call kindly if you need to, and lean on your family afterward.

If he threatens you, damages property or you feel unsafe saying no, your safety comes first. Don’t have this conversation alone, and don’t deliver an ultimatum without a safety plan. The National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233, can help.

## What if I’m afraid saying no will put him in danger?

That fear is real. Neither cash nor a “no” can guarantee his safety, but a few concrete steps help.

- Keep naloxone (Narcan), which can reverse an opioid overdose, at home and learn how to use it. It’s available without a prescription at most U.S. pharmacies.
- If anyone is in immediate danger or you suspect an overdose, call 911. For a mental health or suicide crisis, call or text 988.
- For treatment options, SAMHSA’s National Helpline, 1-800-662-4357, is free, confidential and open 24/7.

Staying connected matters too. A text that says “I love you, and I’m here when you’re ready” costs nothing and keeps the door open.

## How can our family put this into practice together?

Make every money request visible to the family, decide together, and keep a record.

Start with a simple list of what each person has given over the past three months. Seeing the total in one place often changes the conversation.

## How do I take care of myself when saying no feels awful?

Expect guilt, and treat it as a sign of love, not proof that you’re wrong.

Protect your own finances, including savings, retirement and what your other children need. Money drained from your future is money you won’t have if your son becomes ready for treatment. And find people who understand: Al-Anon and Nar-Anon meetings are full of parents who have made this same hard call.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Giving an Addicted Son Money

### What if he’ll be homeless if I don’t pay his rent?

This is one of the hardest calls a parent faces, and families land in different places. Some pay the landlord directly for a short time while their son takes agreed steps. Others offer to help with treatment or sober living instead. Whatever you choose, decide it together and calmly.

### What if he stops speaking to me after I say no?

That hurts, and it’s often temporary. Keep sending short, warm messages that have nothing to do with money. When he reaches out again, welcome him without a lecture.

### What if my spouse keeps giving him money anyway?

Talk privately, without blame, and look for [a plan you can both keep](/blog/parents-disagree-about-addicted-son). It’s rarely about weakness. It’s usually about fear.

### Is it ever okay to give him cash?

That’s a decision for your family to make together. Paying for specific things directly lets you help without wondering where the money went.

## Helping Without Rescuing

Saying no to cash isn’t saying no to your son. It’s saying yes to the kind of help that can move him toward health, and it’s far easier to hold when the whole family agrees.
